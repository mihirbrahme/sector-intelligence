What the work is

The service manages the freight cargo claims lifecycle across shippers, intermediaries, carriers, and cargo underwriters.

The workflow executes across five operational stages:

  1. Intake and triage: Captures damage notices, delivery receipts with notations, commercial invoices, and photos within statutory deadlines.
  2. Liability investigation: Inspects bill of lading terms, driver logs, seal records, and reefer downloads to determine physical causation.
  3. Valuation and defense: Establishes statutory liability limits under Carmack, COGSA, or CMR rules, identifying deductible boundaries.
  4. Subrogation recovery: Files claims against responsible motor carriers, railroads, or ocean lines, pursuing settlement recovery.
  5. Salvage and accounting: Coordinates salvage sales, secures destruction certificates for controlled goods, and establishes loss reserves under ASC 450.

How it differs by model

In Model 1, the desk tenders claims to carrier insurers while blocking unauthorized shipper invoice deductions.

In Model 2, the desk manages multimodal liability, balancing ocean carrier limits against customer claims.

In Model 3, the desk investigates facility inventory shrinkage, handling damage, and bailment liabilities.

In Model 4 and Model 5, the desk defends carriers against inflated claims, evaluating cross-dock handling and cargo deductibles.

In Model 7 and Model 10, the desk investigates rail yard shifts and seawater ingress under maritime limits.

What it is aimed at

  • billing exception leakage: Stops shippers from making unauthorized freight bill short-payments to offset unadjudicated cargo claims. Read what follows as a lens applied to the mechanisms above, not as a mechanism this pattern arrests: Removes contentious cargo damage disputes from frontline operations reps, centralizing legal evidence assembly.
  • carrier-selection liability repricing the cost of cheap capacity (Carrier-selection liability): Tracks carrier loss histories, feeding claim defect data back to carrier qualification desks to eliminate high-risk operators.

Unit of work and its economics

The unit of work is the processed cargo claim file or the recovered subrogation dollar.

Processing a formal claim file costs $45 to $120 offshore, compared to $250 to $500 onshore.

Offshore BPO FTEs in India or the Philippines cost $2,000 to $3,200 monthly, compared to $6,000 to $8,500 onshore.

Structured evidence assembly lifts subrogation recovery from 35% to over 65% of filed losses. Recoveries protect 0.5% to 1.5% of freight value.

Preconditions

Deploying a managed claims desk requires four prerequisites:

  1. Digital intake: Shippers must submit claims via standardized portals with mandatory delivery receipts, invoices, and photos.
  2. Telematics preservation: Operators must download reefer recorder files and trailer telematics within 48 hours of transit exceptions.
  3. Offset restrictions: Shipper agreements must explicitly bar freight payment deductions prior to formal claim adjudication.
  4. Settlement authority: Insurers and management must establish formal financial settlement thresholds for rapid dispute clearance.

Commercial structures

  • Per-transaction BPO: Fixed fee of $50 to $85 per processed cargo claim file.
  • Outcome-based and gainshare: Contingency recovery model earning 15% to 25% of net recovered subrogation dollars from third-party carriers.

What has already failed here

Offshore BPO lift-and-shift of cargo claims desks without commercial authority produced severe breakdowns.

Brokers shifted claims intake offshore without training clerks on evidentiary rules. Clerks forwarded unverified emails to insurers without assembling proof.

Carriers ignored claims until statutory filing windows lapsed. Shippers responded by short-paying millions in freight bills, stranding brokers with irrecoverable losses.

Per exception resolution requires commercial authority not workflow software, recovery demands legal enforcement authority rather than ticketing queues.

Where the hard part sits

The marketing pitch promises streamlined claims recovery. The physical difficulty sits in missing evidence and commercial friction.

Shippers take unilateral deductions from freight remittances before investigations conclude. In multimodal moves, ocean lines, railroads, and truckers dispute where damage occurred.

Without immediate seal records and reefer logs, proving custody transfer liability remains difficult.

Where it applies

In the United States, claims follow the Carmack Amendment, imposing strict carrier liability for actual loss within a nine-month window.

In the United Kingdom and Europe, road claims are capped under the CMR Convention at 8.33 SDR per kilogram, requiring cargo insurance.

In Southeast Asia, fragmented domestic transport laws make legal recovery difficult. Operations rely on local surveys and regional desks in Manila, Bangkok, or Singapore.
