In 90 seconds

What it is

Integrating statutory surveys, shipyard bookings, and charter commitments to eliminate docking bunching, hedge charter-in exposure, and protect vessel revenue

Where it applies

Protected liner shipping / Jones Act non-contiguous trades, International container liner and feeder shipping, Dry bulk ocean logistics and chartering, Energy shipping: LNG, crude, product, offshore FPSO/FSO, Great Lakes and coastal bulk shipping

What the work is

The pattern synchronises marine technical management with commercial chartering desks. It ingests statutory class survey cycles alongside shipyard bookings and voyage schedules.

The system staggers yard entries across cohorts, preventing vessels from clustering in dry dock. When vessels dock, planners model replacement tonnage. Desks execute index-linked fixtures tied to Baltic Exchange indices with collar options.

Technical modules pre-scope steel renewals via ultrasonic thickness gauging 12 months prior to docking to prevent off-hire overruns.

Commercial desks vet counterparties against Baltic Exchange records. If a charterer defaults, automated workflows prepare lien-on-subfreight notices under standard charterparty terms to intercept revenues before vessel arrests occur.

How it differs by model

  • Model 11 (Dry Bulk): Staggering Capesize dockings avoids charter-in exposure (charter-in rate exposure locked at the wrong point in the cycle); Baltic vetting mitigates counterparty default (charter counterparty default interrupts cash and voyage control).
  • Model 10 (Liner): Scheduled strings require fixed weekly departures, inserting replacement feeder tonnage or sliding rotations.
  • Model 13 (Great Lakes Bulk): Dockings compress into the 80-to-90 day winter freeze when the St. Lawrence Seaway closes.

What it is aimed at

  • dry-docking bunching removes revenue days (Dry-docking bunching): Scheduled dockings cluster, stripping revenue days. Algorithmic scheduling spreads survey deadlines, preserving fleet capacity.
  • charter-in rate exposure locked at the wrong point in the cycle (Charter-in rate exposure): Chartering replacement ships during dockings traps operators if spot rates fall. Index-linked fixtures hedge rate exposure.
  • charter counterparty default interrupts cash and voyage control (Charter counterparty default): Counterparties default during freight downturns. The system monitors Baltic credit alerts and executes liens on subfreight.
  • off-hire and technical downtime convert owned fleet into idle capital (Off-hire downtime): Unplanned yard extensions convert vessels into idle capital. Pre-docking ultrasonic surveys minimise unforeseen renewals.
  • fleet age capex cliff raises maintenance before replacement is funded (Fleet age capex cliff): Aging vessels face sudden steel renewals. The pattern models dry-dock capex before yard arrival.

Unit of work and its economics

The unit of work is the vessel revenue-day or dry-docking event.

Unforeseen steel renewal adds.50 to.00 per kilogram of installed plate. Counterparty default causes.0M to.5M in stranded hire and legal costs.

Preconditions

Execution requires four prerequisites:

  1. Class survey integration: Ingesting survey cycles from classification societies.
  2. Ultrasonic thickness gauging: Executing scans to quantify steel renewals before arrival.
  3. Advance yard slot contracts: Securing reservations 12 to 18 months in advance with liquidated damages.
  4. Counterparty credit vetting: Integrating Baltic Exchange fixture databases and credit feeds.

Commercial structures

  • Outcome-based and gainshare: Bonuses tied to verified cuts in fleet off-hire days.

What has already failed here

Algorithmic dry-dock platforms omitting physical shipyard constraints fail repeatedly. As recorded in monolithic ERP and WMS rip and replace, owners managing dry-docking through general enterprise ERPs suffered schedule overruns when software assumed uniform yard labour capacity. When yards suffered steel shortages, rigid schedules collapsed.

Where the hard part sits

The pitch describes automated fleet optimization. The operational reality sits in shipyard basins: - Ballast tank inspections reveal unexpected plate corrosion, doubling planned steel renewal. - Shipyards exploit schedule pressure through high change-order rates for unbudgeted emergency repairs.

Where it applies

  • UK/Europe: Yards comply with EU Ship Recycling rules; desks balance Baltic and Mediterranean repair slots.
  • Singapore: Central hub for technical management, afloat repairs, and Baltic fixture arbitration.
  • Malaysia/Indonesia: Regional yards like MMHE Pasir Gudang offer lower steel rates but face equipment import delays.
  • Australia: Remote routes require planning around long positioning voyages to Asian yards.
