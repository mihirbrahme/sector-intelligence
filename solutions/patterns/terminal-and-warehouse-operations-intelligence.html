In 90 seconds

What it is

Real-time execution analytics over terminal operating systems and warehouse systems to synchronise equipment movements and cap unproductive rehandles

Where it applies

Contract Logistics / 3PL Warehousing, Ports & Terminals, Inland liquid-bulk marine / tank barge, Protected liner shipping / Jones Act non-contiguous trades, International container liner and feeder shipping

What the work is

The pattern deploys an analytical layer over a Terminal Operating System (TOS) or Warehouse Management System (WMS). It ingests telemetry from optical character recognition gates, spreader twist-locks, and gantry cranes.

The engine reconciles physical movements against stowage plans and gate appointments.

In the yard, the pattern models retrieval probabilities using dwell history and customs flags. It assigns incoming boxes to stacks dynamically, preventing buried containers. Supervisors monitor automated queues flagging equipment bottlenecks.

How it differs by model

  • Model 6 (Ports): Focuses on gross crane moves per hour. When yard density exceeds 75%, algorithms restrict ground slots to prevent exponential rehandles.
  • Model 3 (3PL): Focuses on pick lines per labour hour. Logic sequences wave picks and rebalances dock labour based on trailer cut-offs.
  • Model 10 (Liner): Monitors vessel turnaround against berth windows, protecting schedule reliability.
  • Model 9 (Protected Liner): Tied to captive terminal ventures (Matson's 35% SSAT stake). Faster turns protect fixed departures.
  • Model 8 (Liquid Marine): Applies at dock interfaces, monitoring pumping rates to prevent uncompensated delay days.

What it is aimed at

  • berth, yard and landside constraints turn terminal density into delay (Terminal density delay): Yard congestion causes rehandles to compound above 75% utilisation. The pattern predicts retrieval timing, capping rehandles.
  • slot under-utilisation hidden by aggregate TEU growth (Slot under-utilisation): Sluggish crane rates cause vessel delays, forcing blank calls. The pattern stabilises berth productivity, protecting slot utilisation. The pattern automates exception triaging before queues paralyse operations.

Unit of work and its economics

The unit of work is the container move for terminals, and the pick line for warehouses.

Gross crane moves cost $35 to $65 per lift under union agreements. An unproductive yard rehandle costs $15 to $30 in fuel, wear, and labour. Above 80% yard density, rehandle ratios rise from 0.25 to over 0.65 moves per import container, eroding EBITDA margins by 300 to 500 basis points.

Union crane gangs cost $250 to $450 per gang-hour. Crane starvation from ITV delays costs $4.00 to $7.50 per minute of idle hook time.

Preconditions

Automated operations intelligence requires four prerequisites:

  1. Equipment telemetry: Equipment transmits position, fuel burn, and twist-lock status via CAN-bus.
  2. Standardised EDI: Terminals ingest clean BAPLIE stowage plans and gate appointments 24 hours prior to berthing.
  3. Labour rule codification: Encoding contractual rest breaks and gang boundaries into dispatch logic.
  4. Physical yard reconciliation: Reconciling container positions with digital TOS coordinates.

Commercial structures

  • Subscription SaaS: Annual licensing scaled by terminal throughput ($0.15 to $0.35 per lift) or warehouse area.
  • Fixed-price diagnostic then build: A 10-week diagnostic costing $120,000 to $250,000 auditing TOS data.
  • Outcome-based and gainshare: Fees tied to audited reductions in rehandle ratios or crane rate increases.

What has already failed here

Monolithic system replacements failed repeatedly in marine terminals. As recorded in monolithic ERP and WMS rip and replace, port operators attempting to replace core TOS platforms with generic software triggered terminal gridlock (Ports of Auckland). Similarly, passive visibility control towers showed that dashboards lacking automated dispatch fail to reduce yard congestion.

Where the hard part sits

The pitch promises mathematical optimization of port assets. The operational friction sits on the terminal pavement: - Longshore drivers bypass automated dispatch screens, following radio instructions from dock foremen. - Salt air and vibration degrade spreader twist-lock sensors, corrupting live telemetry feeds. - Coastal feeder barges arrive with corrupted BAPLIE files, forcing supervisors into manual intervention.

Where it applies

  • North America: High longshore wages ($40-$55/hr) make idle crane gang reduction valuable; union rules limit automated dispatch.
  • UK/Europe: Automated terminals (Rotterdam, Hamburg) deploy automated cranes; electricity costs demand eco-driving.
  • Singapore: Densest hub (PSA Singapore handled 44.5m TEU in 2025); land constraints make fast crane dispatch existential.
  • Malaysia: Port Klang and PTP balance high volume with lower labour costs, prioritising equipment turnover.
  • Indonesia: Tanjung Priok and Perak face high dwell (3.5-5.0 days); focus sits on customs clearance.
  • Australia: Stevedoring duopoly (Patrick, DP World) monitored by ACCC; berth moves per hour is the primary benchmark.
