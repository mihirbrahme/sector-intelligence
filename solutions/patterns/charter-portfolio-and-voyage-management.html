What the work is

The pattern unifies pre-fixture chartering and post-fixture voyage operations within a single commercial operating system. It calculates prospective voyage profitability across voyage charters, time charters, and contracts of affreightment.

The system models time-charter equivalent earnings in real time. It links fixture desks with Baltic Exchange forward curves, structuring index-linked fixtures with collar options rather than fixing period rates at cycle peaks.

Post-fixture modules ingest port logs, statement of facts (SOF) data, and noon reports. The platform calculates laytime automatically against charterparty clauses, including weather working days and notice of readiness terms.

Commercial desks monitor counterparty risk through Baltic Exchange credit feeds.

How it differs by model

  • Model 11 (Dry Bulk): Operators balance owned fleets with chartered-in tonnage. Desks structure index-linked fixtures against Baltic indices and automate weather laytime calculations.
  • Model 12 (Energy Shipping): Tankers operate under Worldscale rates and Shelltime 4 forms. Systems track pumping warranties, SIRE approvals, and laytime terms.
  • Model 10 (Liner): Operators manage chartered-in container tonnage across scheduled strings, tracking period off-hire clauses and redelivery windows.

What it is aimed at

  • charter-in rate exposure locked at the wrong point in the cycle (Charter-in rate exposure): Locking long-term charters at cycle peaks traps operators when spot markets fall. Index-linked fixtures hedge rate exposure across cycles.
  • charter counterparty default interrupts cash and voyage control (Charter counterparty default): Charterers default when rates plunge. Baltic vetting, parent guarantees, and automated liens on subfreight protect freight revenue.
  • off-hire and technical downtime convert owned fleet into idle capital (Off-hire downtime): Speed and consumption disputes strip voyage margin. Automated noon report ingestion detects performance claims early.
  • bunker and carbon pass-through clause mismatch (Bunker and carbon pass-through mismatch): BAF and EU ETS clauses cause billing friction. Real-time voyage carbon accounting validates pass-through claims.

Preconditions

Deployment requires four prerequisites:

  1. Charterparty recap standardisation: Codifying CP forms into machine-readable terms using NYPE or Shelltime libraries.
  2. Statement of facts ingestion: Electronic SOF capture with automated OCR extraction of port events.
  3. Baltic Exchange credit feeds: Connecting platforms to live Baltic Exchange fixture and credit APIs.
  4. Vessel telematics connectivity: Ingesting noon reports and sensor telemetry tracking speed and bunker consumption.

What has already failed here

Decoupling paper freight derivatives from physical vessel positions causes corporate collapses. As documented in monolithic ERP and WMS rip and replace, operators treating Forward Freight Agreements as speculative bets suffered margin liquidations when Baltic indices spiked (e.g. Britannia Bulk). Similarly, generic ERP tools attempting to handle maritime laytime fail because they cannot model complex nautical exceptions.

Where the hard part sits

The pitch describes automated voyage management. The operational reality sits in contentious contractual ambiguity: - Exercising liens on subfreight requires immediate legal notices to sub-charterers before freight payments clear offshore escrow accounts. - Speed and consumption warranties depend on disputed weather routing data, leading to protracted maritime arbitration.

Where it applies

  • North America: US Gulf bulk and tanker exports require handling Mississippi River draft restrictions and bar pilot laytime rules.
  • UK/Europe: London is the maritime arbitration capital (LMAA); contracts reference English maritime law and EU ETS rules.
  • Singapore: Central chartering hub for Asian trades, using Singapore Chamber of Maritime Arbitration clauses.
  • Malaysia/Indonesia: High-volume bulk exports present recurring demurrage disputes and barging congestion delays.
  • Australia: Major bulk loading ports (Port Hedland, Hay Point) enforce strict terminal laytime rules and berthing windows.
