What the work is

The pattern applies mathematical revenue management to container liner strings. It replaces static branch allocation quotas with algorithmic slot valuation engines connected directly to booking desks.

The software models multidimensional capacity constraints per voyage leg: nominal TEU slots, deadweight tonnage (DWT), reefer plug availability, and dangerous goods segregation rules. Cargo that weighs out a vessel before filling slots is penalised unless freight rates compensate for displaced capacity.

Algorithms calculate network contribution margin rather than gross leg revenue.

Commercial interfaces adjust spot rates dynamically. Booking requests from forwarders and BCOs are screened against historical show-up probabilities, applying booking fees to deter ghost bookings.

How it differs by model

  • Model 10 (Liner): Global carriers optimize multi-port strings, balancing high-rate headhaul cargo against low-rate backhaul cargo and empty repositioning slots.
  • Model 9 (Protected Liner): Jones Act carriers face captive trade lanes with structural volume imbalances, requiring strict allocation for high-paying retail freight.
  • Model 2 (Forwarding/NVOCC): NVOCCs act as slot buyers, optimizing co-loading margins across contracted minimum quantity commitments and carrier spot market space.

Unit of work and its economics

The unit of work is the twenty-foot equivalent unit (TEU) slot on a specific voyage leg.

Headhaul spot revenue on 2026-09-10 ran $7,352 per 40ft Shanghai–Los Angeles and $9,726 Shanghai–New York, against $3,997 Shanghai–Rotterdam and a composite of $4,476.

The spread between those four numbers is the yield problem in one line. A slot on the same vessel class is worth 2.4x more on Shanghai–New York than on Shanghai–Rotterdam in the same week. An allocation engine priced off the composite is pricing off a number that describes no lane the carrier actually sells. The composite was flat for a second week while transpacific firmed and Asia–Europe fell — the stability is an artifact of averaging two opposite movements.

Empty repositioning cost per move is not recorded here: see mechanism library Gaps, where the absence of a mechanism for empty asset movement is logged. The sizing that does exist is 12% to 20%+ of liner operating costs.

The weight-versus-slot trade is the mechanism in miniature. A vessel that reaches its deadweight limit before its slots are full has sold volume it cannot carry. Accepting one heavy low-rate container — scrap paper, steel, liquids in flexitanks — displaces two light high-rate containers when the vessel drafts out. At the Shanghai–Los Angeles rate above, displacing two boxes to carry one costs roughly $7,350 on a single slot decision, and the booking desk that made it saw only the revenue it gained.

No-show rates without commitment fees run between 15% and 30%.

Preconditions

Deployment requires four prerequisites:

  1. Booking and manifest integration: EDI integration with booking platforms and stowage tools.
  2. Equipment imbalance visibility: Real-time tracking of empty container inventory and depot capacity.
  3. Weight verification compliance: Ingesting verified gross mass certificates under SOLAS rules.
  4. Historical show-up rate tracking: Compiling shipper no-show records to calibrate overbooking parameters.

What has already failed here

Transposing passenger airline yield management directly to ocean shipping has failed repeatedly. As documented in monolithic ERP and WMS rip and replace, early revenue tools treated slots like airplane seats.

Airline seats do not impose empty return costs after passengers disembark. Systems optimizing spot bookings solely on leg margin caused severe empty box deficits in Asian export hubs, forcing carriers to charter expensive extra-loader vessels to reposition empty equipment.

Where the hard part sits

The pitch describes algorithmic dynamic pricing. The operational reality sits in physical maritime constraints: - Shippers declare inaccurate container weights at booking, causing vessels to reach deadweight draft limits before slots are full. - Customer relationships with beneficial cargo owners restrict aggressive dynamic pricing on annual service contracts. - Port omissions and sudden blank sailings invalidate calculated slot allocations across subsequent network legs.

Where it applies

  • North America: High-volume import gateways (LA/Long Beach) generate empty container surpluses requiring systematic evacuation allocations.
  • UK/Europe: Intermodal rail connectivity constraints dictate inland slot handoffs at Rotterdam and Hamburg.
  • Singapore: World\'s largest transshipment hub; algorithms balance connecting feeder slots against mainline vessel stowage.
  • Malaysia/Indonesia: High export volumes from Port Klang and Tanjung Pelepas require strict feeder slot allocation.
  • Australia: Severe trade imbalances on southbound goods require aggressive backhaul pricing to encourage agri-bulk exports.
