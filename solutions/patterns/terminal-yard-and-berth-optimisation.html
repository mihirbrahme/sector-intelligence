In 90 seconds

What it is

Algorithmic co-optimisation of vessel berth allocation, quay crane assignment, and container yard stacking logic to eliminate ship idle time and unproductive box rehandles

Where it applies

Ports & Terminals, International container liner and feeder shipping

What the work is

The pattern coordinates seaside and yard operations within maritime container terminals. It links the berth allocation problem (BAP) with the quay crane scheduling problem (QCSP) and yard stacking logic.

Dynamic planning engines ingest automated vessel arrival forecasts via AIS telematics. The system dynamically assigns quay frontage and crane splits, matching vessel draft and outreach requirements against physical berth capabilities.

Yard stacking modules calculate container placement in real time. Rather than stacking boxes purely by physical convenience, algorithms ingest export stowage plans, booking data, and expected truck arrival windows.

Containers are marshalled into yard blocks to match ship crane work sequences. This sequencing minimises unburying moves, where cranes must shift top containers to retrieve buried target boxes.

How it differs by model

  • Model 6 (Ports): Terminal operators maximize berth moves per hour (BMPH) and crane productivity (GMPH), preventing yard congestion above 75% utilization.
  • Model 10 (Liner): Liner carriers require guaranteed berth windows. Turnaround delays force vessels to speed up between ports, burning millions in compensatory bunker fuel.

What it is aimed at

  • berth, yard and landside constraints turn terminal density into delay (Berth, yard and landside constraints turn density into delay): Yard density above 75% causes exponential reshuffling. Predictive stacking logic maintains yard velocity.
  • port congestion breaks liner rotation integrity: Off-window vessel arrivals trigger berth queues. Dynamic reallocation absorbs schedule disruptions.
  • detention and demurrage under-recovery (Empty miles / repositioning deadweight): Misplaced empty boxes create unproductive shifts. Automated yard blocks segregate empties by carrier release order.

Unit of work and its economics

The unit of work is the container crane move, yard rehandle, or vessel port call.

Terminal quay cranes target 28 to 35 gross crane moves per hour (GMPH), delivering 80 to 140 berth moves per hour across multi-crane gangs.

Every unproductive yard rehandle costs to in direct diesel, equipment wear, and operator labour. In a terminal handling 1.5 million TEU annually, an unoptimised yard with a 1.8 rehandle ratio generates over million in avoidable shifting costs.

Preconditions

Deployment requires four prerequisites:

  1. Terminal Operating System digitisation: Real-time TOS data feeds from platforms like Navis N4, Tideworks, or CyberLogitec.
  2. Equipment telematics connectivity: Position detection systems (DGPS) mounted on RTGs, straddle carriers, and terminal tractors.
  3. Gate appointment integration: Synchronised truck appointment systems smoothing landside collection and drop-off spikes.

Commercial structures

  • Outcome-based and gainshare: Bonuses tied to verified gains in gross crane moves per hour and reduced truck dwell.

What has already failed here

Automated stacking algorithms designed in academic simulations collapse when physical yard density exceeds 75%. As documented in monolithic ERP and WMS rip and replace, early automation suites assumed uniform yard operations.

When terminal occupancy surpassed 80%, automated stacking cranes spent their operating cycles reshuffling containers to unbury buried boxes. This generated severe quay crane starvation, ground gridlock, and vessel berth overruns.

Where the hard part sits

The pitch describes automated yard sequencing. The operational reality sits in physical port volatility: - Vessels arrive 24 to 48 hours off-window, forcing planners to scrap pre-marshalled yard blocks and re-stow entire vessel bays. - Truck turn times surge when drayage drivers arrive unannounced with incorrect documentation, blocking yard transfer points. - High winds force quay crane shut-downs, invalidating calculated crane split schedules across all berthed tonnage.

Where it applies

  • North America: West Coast ports (Los Angeles, Long Beach) face severe landside chassis shortages and strict ILWU work rules.
  • UK/Europe: Automated hubs (Rotterdam, Hamburg) deploy automated guided vehicles (AGVs) requiring millimetre-precise container sequencing.
  • Singapore: World\'s largest transshipment gateway (PSA Tuas); operations require massive multi-vessel yard-to-berth connection optimisation.
  • Malaysia/Indonesia: Mega-terminals (Tanjung Pelepas, Port Klang) manage high transshipment densities alongside intermodal rail corridors.
  • Australia: Ports (Melbourne, Botany) operate under strict environmental noise caps and tight terminal land footprint boundaries.
