What the work is

The pattern centralises equipment lifecycle management for containers, chassis, and gensets. It ingests EDI 322 gate events, EDI 301 bookings, depot OCR scans, and GPS telematics.

The engine reconciles physical container statuses against free-time limits. It identifies containers approaching free-time expiry and matches them with export bookings for street turns, avoiding drayage to congested marine terminals.

Depot modules forecast regional box deficits. Predictive algorithms schedule empty repositioning on liner backhaul voyages or rail repo trains before inland depots stock out.

Equipment controllers stop managing inventory on disconnected spreadsheets. Automated workflows trigger depot off-hire returns when equipment reaches contract milestones.

How it differs by model

  • Model 10 (Liner): Manages global box imbalances. Repositioning empty boxes from import deficits to Asian hubs represents 12% to 20%+ of operating costs.
  • Model 7 (Domestic Intermodal): Governs owned 53-ft containers (such as J.B. Hunt's 115k boxes). Prioritises container turns per year (target 18-22 turns) over rail lines.
  • Model 2 (Forwarding): Non-asset NVOCCs track equipment free-time calendars and interchange receipts (EIR) to prevent carrier per-diem penalties.
  • Model 4 (Asset Carriage): Focuses on chassis street turns, appointment availability, and roadworthiness inspections under UIIA rules.

What it is aimed at

  • detention and demurrage under-recovery: Margins leak when containers dwell past free-time. Automated tracking generates verified interchange logs, disputing invalid per-diem invoices under OSRA 22.
  • uncontrolled purchased input behaving as a fixed cost (Uncontrolled purchased input): Intermodal carriers face rising chassis pool fees. Asset optimization maximises owned chassis utilisation, minimising third-party lease expenses.
  • off-hire and technical downtime convert owned fleet into idle capital (Off-hire downtime): Damaged containers sit idle in depot yards. Digitising IICL damage surveys accelerates repair approvals and returns boxes to revenue service.

Unit of work and its economics

The unit of work is the equipment-day (container-day, chassis-day) or empty repositioning move.

Empty container repositioning costs $150 to $450 per TEU moved (Drewry 2025). Third-party chassis rentals run $30 to $45 per day in US ports. Newbuild container capex averages $2,000 to $3,200 per forty-foot box (FEU).

Executing street turns saves $200 to $500 per container by avoiding dual empty drayage moves and terminal gate fees.

Preconditions

Distributed equipment asset management requires four critical prerequisites:

  1. Depot EDI standardization: Connecting inland depots via CEDEX and EDI 322 messaging.
  2. Standardised M&R: Implementing digital IICL inspection workflows at depot gates.
  3. Automated gate integration: Connecting carrier systems to terminal booking portals.
  4. Master equipment reconciliation: Reconciling container numbers against physical yard inventories.

Commercial structures

  • Subscription SaaS: Monthly fees per managed container or chassis asset ($0.50 to $1.50 per TEU/month).
  • Per-transaction managed service or BPO: Fees for auditing interchange receipts and repair estimates ($5 to $15 per file).
  • Recovery fee contingency: Contingency fees (15% to 25%) tied to disputed per-diem charge recovery.

What has already failed here

Consortium blockchain trade platforms failed to solve equipment positioning.

As analyzed in consortium blockchain trade documentation, TradeLens attempted to track global container assets using distributed ledgers. The project failed because carriers refused to share proprietary asset data with competitors.

Similarly, passive visibility control towers proved that displaying container GPS dots without street-turn matching logic does nothing to reduce empty repositioning expenses.

Where the hard part sits

The pitch promises automated global container balance.

The operational difficulty lies in structural trade deficits: - Macroeconomic trade imbalances cannot be coded away; Transpacific headhaul volumes structurally outnumber backhaul cargo by nearly two to one. - Ocean carriers hoard empty containers in export regions during market rallies, refusing to release boxes for domestic street turns. - Drayage drivers resist off-dock depot returns when depot hours are shorter than main terminal gates.

Where it applies

  • North America: Chassis rules (UIIA) and FMC Rules (46 CFR Part 541) make automated timestamps essential.
  • UK/Europe: Dominated by merchant haulage; equipment tracking focuses on inland dry-port dwell.
  • Singapore: Central transhipment hub; high box turnover demands tight lease off-hire execution in free-trade zones.
  • Malaysia: Port Klang and PTP serve as empty storage yards for regional feeder strings.
  • Indonesia: Cabotage laws mandate domestic equipment; inter-island imbalances create empty box accumulation in outports.
  • Australia: Long interstate rail corridors create container balance challenges between eastern cities and Perth.
