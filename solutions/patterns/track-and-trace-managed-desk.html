What the work is

The service manages the in-transit visibility lifecycle, bridging passive tracking tools and active dispatch intervention.

Five core workflows execute across operations:

  1. Driver onboarding: Enforces driver tracking app adoption and verifies electronic logging device (ELD) data feeds prior to pickup.
  2. Active milestone check-calls: Contacts dispatchers and drivers at critical milestones (arrival, loading departure, transit midpoint, consignee arrival).
  3. Telemetry exception clearing: Resolves broken geofences, lost GPS signals, and dwell alerts generated by platforms.
  4. Terminal milestone scraping: Monitors marine terminal systems and railroad portals for vessel berthing, ramp groundings, and demurrage cut-offs.
  5. Proof-of-delivery collection: Retrieves signed bills of lading within hours of delivery completion to initiate carrier settlement.

How it differs by model

In Model 1, the desk enforces driver tracking compliance, identifying missed pickups early to enable load recovery before spot rate inflation.

In Model 2, the desk tracks ocean containers, monitoring vessel arrival schedules, terminal gate events, and customs clearance releases.

In Model 4, the desk monitors fleet driver hours of service against delivery appointment windows, alerting dispatch to reroute delayed tractors.

In Model 7, the desk tracks rail well-car interchange, chassis usage, and ramp dwell before storage fees accrue.

In Model 10, the desk monitors container voyages, tracking port congestion and berth availability across rotations.

What it is aimed at

  • detention and demurrage under-recovery (Deadhead and empty repositioning): Early delivery milestone verification allows fleet planners to book backhauls and eliminate empty repositioning miles. Read what follows as a lens applied to the mechanisms above, not as a mechanism this pattern arrests: Offloads repetitive check-calls and tracking exceptions from senior brokers and dispatchers, eliminating hidden operational costs.

Unit of work and its economics

The unit of work is the tracked shipment lifecycle or active milestone check-call.

Shipment tracking costs range from $4.00 to $8.50 per shipment offshore, compared to $16.00 to $28.00 in onshore operations.

Offshore dedicated FTE seats in India or the Philippines cost $2,200 to $3,500 monthly, compared to $5,500 to $8,000 onshore.

Deflecting routine tracking tasks increases broker load volume capacity by 35% to 50%.

Preconditions

Deploying a tracking desk requires four core elements:

  1. Visibility platform foundation: Aggregation software (Project44, FourKites, MacroPoint) connecting carrier ELD telemetry to dispatch systems.
  2. Standardized escalation triggers: Documented operational thresholds defining when transit delays escalate to commercial brokerage teams.
  3. Carrier compliance terms: Broker-carrier agreements establishing mandatory tracking adoption and rate deductions for untracked loads.
  4. Milestone status taxonomy: Standard definitions of arrival, departure, in-transit, and exception states across all transport modes.

Commercial structures

  • Per-transaction BPO: Fixed fee of $4.00 to $7.50 per tracked shipment with defined tracking percentage SLAs.
  • Dedicated FTE monthly desk: Fixed charge of $2,400 to $3,600 per full-time equivalent tracking agent.
  • Hybrid platform plus service fee: Combined software access fee and variable per-load charge for exception-only check-calls.

What has already failed here

Outsourced tracking desks operating purely as human telephone callers represent expensive, non-value-adding operational overhead.

When drivers ignore calls, offshore desks log unreachable status and take no operational action.

Without spending authority to dispatch rescue power or reschedule appointments, tracking becomes a post-mortem logging exercise.

As analyzed in passive visibility and control towers, passive tracking without operational execution authority creates zero commercial value.

Where the hard part sits

Operational difficulty sits in carrier resistance, data fragmentation, and app dropouts.

Independent owner-operators frequently refuse to install mobile tracking apps or disable location permissions due to phone battery and privacy concerns.

ELD data feeds disconnect mid-transit, generating false delay alerts that overwhelm tracking queues.

Desks spend excessive working hours chasing uncooperative drivers rather than resolving genuine transport delays.

Where it applies

In the United States, the ELD mandate (FMCSA 49 CFR Part 395) enables direct telematics feeds, though driver app privacy concerns remain high.

In the United Kingdom and Europe, strict GDPR regulations restrict driver smartphone tracking, and cross-border roaming dropouts disrupt data feeds.

In Southeast Asia, fleets rely on proprietary vehicle black-boxes and messaging apps, with minimal centralized ELD integration.
