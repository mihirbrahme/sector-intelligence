Heavy equipment and terminal fleet optimisation

In 90 seconds

What it is

Telemetry-driven dispatch, job-stepping, and condition monitoring for gantry cranes and terminal vehicles to eliminate empty travel and prevent technical downtime

Where it applies

Ports & Terminals, Inland liquid-bulk marine / tank barge, Dry bulk ocean logistics and chartering, Great Lakes and coastal bulk shipping

What the work is

The pattern connects container handling equipment (CHE) to an operational telemetry engine. It captures CAN-bus engine diagnostics, J1939 protocols, hydraulic curves, and positioning data from gantry cranes (RTGs), reachstackers, and internal transfer vehicles (ITVs).

The engine assigns tasks using proximity algorithms and spreader twist-lock states. Rather than returning empty to central yards, ITVs receive opportunistic transfer dispatches between cranes and container stacks.

Condition monitoring algorithms analyze vibration and temperature signatures on crane gearboxes and diesel generators, detecting degradation before structural failure occurs.

Supervisors stop reacting to breakdowns mid-shift. Automated schedules route machines to repair bays during vessel lulls.

How it differs by model

  • Model 6 (Ports): Focuses on RTG and ITV utilisation. Dynamic job-stepping cuts ITV empty travel by 20% to 35%, preventing crane starvation.
  • Model 8 (Liquid Marine): Applies to towboat horsepower and fleeting. Telemetry tracks engine load factors and hydraulics to prevent breakdowns.
  • Model 11 (Dry Bulk): Focuses on ship unloaders and conveyor belts. Monitors abrasive dust contamination and bearing heat to prevent conveyor halts.
  • Model 13 (Great Lakes Bulk): Governs self-unloader boom equipment and hydraulics. Prioritises repairs before the 275-day navigation window opens.

What it is aimed at

  • off-hire and technical downtime convert owned fleet into idle capital (Off-hire downtime): Unplanned equipment breakdowns halt terminal gangs. Predictive condition monitoring detects hoist and hydraulic wear, scheduling repairs outside active operations.
  • fleet age capex cliff raises maintenance before replacement is funded (Fleet age capex cliff): Older yard equipment suffers escalating maintenance costs. Predictive servicing extends operational machine life by 2 to 4 years.
  • berth, yard and landside constraints turn terminal density into delay (Terminal density delay): Breakdowns during peak calls paralyse yard throughput. Optimising fleet availability maintains crane productivity during density spikes.

Unit of work and its economics

The unit of work is the equipment operating hour or container lift.

A diesel RTG consumes 18 to 25 litres of diesel per operating hour ($20 to $30/hr). Unscheduled RTG failure idling a crane gang costs $250 to $450 per gang-hour in stevedoring downtime. Mechanical maintenance costs run $12 to $22 per operating hour across aged diesel fleets.

Dynamic job-stepping cuts ITV fuel consumption by 15% to 25%, saving $4,000 to $7,000 per tractor annually.

Preconditions

Fleet optimisation requires four essential prerequisites:

  1. Multi-OEM telemetry: Standardising CAN-bus data pipelines across mixed fleets (Kalmar, Konecranes, ZPMC).
  2. Wireless connectivity: Deploying private LTE or Wi-Fi mesh across container stacks to prevent packet loss.
  3. Driver terminal interfaces: Installing ruggedized vehicle-mounted terminals for automated dispatch.
  4. Maintenance records digitization: Migrating paper logs into structured digital maintenance databases.

Commercial structures

  • Subscription SaaS: Annual licensing per tracked machine ($350 to $750 per asset).
  • Fixed-price diagnostic then build: A 12-week diagnostic costing $100,000 to $200,000 auditing telematics data.
  • Outcome-based and gainshare: Fees linked to audited fuel burn reductions or verified MTBF increases.

What has already failed here

Automated yard equipment projects have encountered major operational setbacks.

As shown in autonomous long haul trucking commercialisation, retrofitting complex autonomous kits onto legacy diesel equipment failed due to base mechanical breakdowns. In terminals, automated guided vehicles deployed without continuous condition monitoring halted operations when minor hydraulic leaks went undetected.

Similarly, passive visibility control towers proved that telematics maps showing equipment locations without automated work dispatch produce zero operational savings.

Where the hard part sits

The pitch promises automated autonomous fleet dispatch.

The operational reality involves physical degradation: - Marine salt spray and vibration corrode CAN-bus connectors and antenna mounts within 12 months. - Equipment drivers resist automated job-stepping screens, preferring static queue lines under specific cranes. - Maintenance supervisors clash with operations teams over pulling machines for preventative servicing during busy ship days.

Where it applies

  • North America: High longshore labour rates ($40-$55/hr) make equipment uptime essential; CARB rules accelerate electric CHE retrofits.
  • UK/Europe: High power and diesel taxes drive electric RTG adoption and energy monitoring.
  • Singapore: Densest transhipment hub (PSA Singapore); automated AGV fleets at Tuas Port require millimetre dispatch.
  • Malaysia: Port Klang and PTP balance high volumes with lower wages, focusing on diesel life extension.
  • Indonesia: Tanjung Priok and Perak operate aged fleets; telematics focus on preventing gearbox failures.
  • Australia: Stevedoring duopoly (Patrick, DP World) faces strict rules; automated fatigue telemetry is mandatory.
