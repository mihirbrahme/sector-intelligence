In 90 seconds

What it is

Algorithmic load-matching platforms failed to replace traditional freight brokers because digital brokerage remains a working-capital-intensive credit and risk-underwriting business, not a software platform with zero marginal cost

Where it applies

Truckload Brokerage

What the promise was

Digital freight brokerages promised to automate truckload booking through two-sided algorithmic marketplaces.

They claimed automated pricing and instantaneous matching would eliminate human broker commissions, drive software-style gross margins, and achieve self-reinforcing network effects.

Advocates projected operating ratios below 80% by replacing traditional floor reps with automated dispatch.

Who made it

  • Convoy: Founded 2015; raised over $900 million in equity and debt; valued at $3.8 billion in April 2022.
  • Uber Freight: Launched 2017; received $500 million from Greenbriar Equity Group (2020); acquired Transplace for $1.8 billion (2021).
  • Transfix: Founded 2013; raised over $130 million; attempted a $1.1 billion SPAC merger (cancelled 2022).

What actually happened

  • Convoy collapsed abruptly on 2023-10-19: The company ceased operations, cancelled outstanding loads, and laid off approximately 500 remaining staff without a formal Chapter 11 filing. Hercules Capital, holding senior secured debt, initiated a foreclosure process. Flexport acquired Convoy's intellectual property in November 2023 for a reported low eight-figure sum, later selling the asset to DAT Freight & Analytics in July 2025.
  • Transfix abandoned brokerage: On 2024-05-01, Transfix announced the sale of its core freight brokerage operations to NFI Industries, pivoting exclusively to software licensing.
  • Uber Freight accumulated continuous operating losses: In Uber Technologies, Inc. Form 10-K filings, the Freight segment reported Adjusted EBITDA losses of $135 million (FY2022), $244 million (FY2023), and continued operating deficits through FY2024.

Why it failed in operational rather than narrative terms

1. Working capital asymmetry scaled with gross revenue

Under operating model 1, carriers demand settlement within 2 to 15 days via quick-pay options. Enterprise shippers settle on 45 to 60-day terms.

Receivables scale with gross revenue, while earnings scale with net revenue.

When spot rates inflated in 2021, cash burn expanded dramatically. When rates collapsed in 2022–2023, freight volumes declined while fixed tech overhead remained. As detailed in venture-subsidised pricing masks negative broker unit economics, investor equity subsidised negative gross spreads to simulate automated marketplace liquidity.

2. Carrier switching costs are zero

Venture models assumed two-sided network density: more shippers attract more carriers, lowering buy rates.

In reality, US truckload capacity is hyper-fragmented; over 80% of carriers operate six or fewer tractors.

Independent owner-operators exhibit zero platform loyalty. A carrier will switch apps for a $25 linehaul difference. Density failed to produce rate discounts below prevailing DAT spot rates.

3. The exception trap

Automated matching functions cleanly on standard "happy path" loads.

However, 15% to 25% of truckload freight encounters operational exceptions: missed loading appointments, driver detention, lumper fees, equipment defects, or load cancellations.

Per happy path automation leaves unit costs scaling with exceptions, exceptions cannot clear algorithmic workflows. They drop into manual queues.

Convoy and its peers had to hire large back-office operations teams, causing personnel costs per load to track incumbent broker averages.

4. Freight fraud and carrier-selection liabilities

Open digital onboarding created massive vulnerability to carrier identity theft, load phishing, and unauthorized double-brokering.

Rising litigation liabilities under carrier-selection liability repricing the cost of cheap capacity forced platforms to restrict automated dispatch and implement manual compliance screening. This eliminated automated throughput advantages.

Why the mechanisms are harder to fix than they look

  • quote-to-book manual handoff: Digital brokers proved order entry and tender acceptance can be automated via API. But they proved that booking is not where brokerage labour resides. The stubborn labour sink is exception resolution and appointment rescheduling. Automating the booking touchpoint saves four minutes, while resolving a missed dock appointment consumes forty.
  • manual carrier sourcing under rate volatility: Algorithmic pricing tools reacted to historical market moves rather than forward supply shifts. During market inflections, pricing algorithms consistently underpriced shipper commitments and overpaid for spot cover, generating negative net revenue spreads.

What the sector learned

  1. Brokerage is a commercial credit and execution business: Shippers do not pay brokers for algorithmic matching. They pay brokers to absorb capacity risk, guarantee pickup, and finance working capital.
  2. Incumbents absorb digital features cheaply: Legacy brokers (C.H. Robinson Worldwide, Inc., RXO, Inc.) built or acquired automated booking APIs without sustaining venture-scale equity dilution or debt overhang. Digital features became table stakes, not competitive moats.

How a credible adviser opens a conversation

Never pitch "disrupting traditional freight brokerage" or "end-to-end autonomous dispatch."

Open on the specific friction points that broke digital broker unit economics: - "What percentage of your digitally booked loads fall out of automated workflows into manual exception handling?" - "How do you reconcile carrier quick-pay cash outflows against 60-day shipper receivables during rate inflections?" - "How has tightened carrier safety vetting under carrier-selection liability repricing the cost of cheap capacity affected your digital booking throughput?"

Where this appears elsewhere
