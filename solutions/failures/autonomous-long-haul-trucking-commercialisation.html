Autonomous long-haul trucking driverless commercialisation

In 90 seconds

What it is

Venture-backed autonomous trucking firms failed to achieve commercial driverless operations on interstate highways because the capital cost of transfer-hub drayage and safety liability exceeded driver wage savings

Where it applies

Asset Carriage / Truckload Fleet

What the promise was

Autonomous trucking developers promised Level 4 driverless Class 8 tractors running commercial linehaul across highway networks by 2021–2024.

Proponents argued removing the driver would eliminate 35% to 40% of fleet operating cost per mile, bypass FMCSA 11-hour Hours of Service limits, and enable 20-hour daily tractor utilisation.

Who made it

  • TuSimple: Founded 2015; raised over $1.3 billion; completed Nasdaq IPO in April 2021 at an $8.5 billion valuation.
  • Embark Trucks: Founded 2016; raised over $300 million; completed a SPAC transaction in November 2021 at a $5.2 billion valuation.
  • Starsky Robotics: Founded 2015; raised $20 million to commercialize teleoperated driverless trucks.
  • Locomation: Founded 2018; developed autonomous follower-truck platooning systems.

What actually happened

  • Starsky Robotics shut down in March 2020: The company ceased operations after failing to secure a Series B round. Founder Stefan Seltz-Axmacher documented that testing costs with safety drivers ran at over $3.00 per mile against an industry market rate of $1.80 per mile.
  • Embark Trucks liquidated in 2023: In March 2023, Embark laid off 70% of staff and initiated a wind-down. In May 2023, Applied Intuition acquired the company in an all-cash merger for $71 million, representing a 99% decline from its SPAC valuation.
  • TuSimple delisted from Nasdaq in January 2024: On 2024-01-29, TuSimple filed Form 25 with the SEC to voluntarily delist common stock. Following federal investigations (CFIUS, FBI, SEC) into technology transfer, TuSimple abandoned US autonomous trucking operations and liquidated its US tractor fleet.
  • Commercial reality: Driverless Class 8 freight (operating without human safety drivers or chase vehicles) represents functionally 0.0% of commercial truckload freight tonnage in the United States.

Why it failed in operational rather than narrative terms

1. The transfer-hub penalty and drayage friction

Class 8 long-haul autonomy is strictly confined to interstate highways (its Operational Design Domain).

Autonomous tractors cannot navigate gravel shipper yards, tight urban dock doors, or complex industrial parks.

This constraint requires a "hub-to-hub" operating model: human drivers haul trailers from shippers to highway transfer yards, uncouple, hook to an autonomous tractor for highway linehaul, uncouple at a destination yard, and hook to a local drayage tractor for final delivery.

Each transfer adds yard dwell, hostler labor, and local drayage costs ($150 to $300 per leg).

Under operating model 4, adding two drayage legs and two yard handoffs completely eliminates the linehaul driver-wage savings on hauls under 800 miles.

2. High sensor capex and highway wear

Equipping a Class 8 tractor with automotive-ompute, redundant steering, braking actuators, radar, and lidar added $100,000 to $150,000 to a standard $150,000 tractor purchase price.

Highway road vibrations, bugs, gravel, mud, and weather extremes routinely degraded optical sensors, requiring frequent manual recalibration and roadside maintenance.

Equipment depreciation and maintenance costs per mile increased faster than driver compensation declined.

3. Asymmetric safety-driver economics during R&D

Achieving autonomous validation required millions of test miles.

Federal and state safety rules required two paid technicians (a safety driver and an AV software engineer) in every test cab.

Carriers incurred double personnel expense while hauling low-yield commercial freight, burning through cash reserves without achieving driverless unit economics.

4. Uninsurable nuclear verdict exposure

Under carrier-selection liability repricing the cost of cheap capacity, asset carriers face severe nuclear verdicts in commercial truck accident litigation.

In commercial litigation, any mechanical or software failure resulting in a fatality triggers punitive damage claims.

Commercial insurers refused to write primary liability or excess umbrella policies for fully unmanned 80,000-pound vehicles without safety drivers, creating an insurmountable balance-sheet barrier.

Why the mechanism is harder to fix than it looks

  • carrier-selection liability repricing the cost of cheap capacity: Autonomous trucking advocates treated safety as a purely probabilistic software challenge (e.g., mean miles between disengagements). But under US tort law, liability is not probabilistic; an unmanned system that causes an accident cannot be cross-examined or assert human error mitigation. The liability exposure transfers directly to the motor carrier and the technology vendor, pricing the insurance premium above human driver wages.

What the sector learned

  1. Closed-loop environments precede open highways: Automation is commercially viable in confined, low-speed environments—such as autonomous yard hostlers moving trailers between dock doors at 10 mph—where ODD boundaries are fixed and highway collision risks are absent.
  2. Active safety assist delivers immediate ROI: Fleets adopt Level 2 driver-assist technology (automated emergency braking, lane keep, forward collision radar) because it lowers insurance premiums and driver fatigue without requiring regulatory approvals or transfer-hub infrastructure.

How a credible adviser opens a conversation

Never pitch "Level 4 highway autonomy" to an asset carrier.

Open on the operational metrics that define fleet profitability: - "What is your current cost per mile for driver recruitment, onboarding, and turnover?" - "How many tractor-hours per week are lost to terminal yard dwell and dock congestion?" - "What excess liability insurance premiums are you paying per tractor under recent carrier-selection liability repricing the cost of cheap capacity litigation exposure?"
