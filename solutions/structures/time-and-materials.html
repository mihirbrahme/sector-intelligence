In 90 seconds

What it is

A flexible engagement structure where the buyer pays for professional engineering and advisory hours worked at agreed rate cards, typically structured in two-week sprints with budget caps

How it works mechanically

The provider bills for actual labor hours expended by designated roles (solution architect, data engineer, integration developer) against an agreed rate schedule.

Work is organized into iterative agile sprints (typically two-week cadences).

Each sprint opens with sprint planning to estimate backlog story points and closes with a sprint review demonstrating completed code or data pipelines.

In enterprise logistics contracts, T&M engagements typically carry a "Not-to-Exceed" (NTE) monthly budget cap or milestone ceiling.

The client retains the right to pause or terminate the engagement upon 14 to 30 days written notice.

What it suits

  • Quote-to-book workflow automation addressing quote-to-book manual handoff: Adapting custom APIs to unique legacy shipper TMS interfaces.
  • Carrier and customer master data consolidation addressing fragmented carrier master after acquisition: Cleaning unpredictable legacy entity records.
  • Event-driven visibility data pipeline addressing billing exception leakage and detention and demurrage under-recovery: Building bespoke webhook connectors across non-standard carrier telematics.

Risk allocation

  • Buyer risk: High. The client absorbs all delivery and budget risk. If technical integration takes twice as long as anticipated due to legacy software debt, the buyer pays for all additional hours.
  • Provider risk: Low. The provider is guaranteed payment for time expended, regardless of whether the client's operational metrics improve or the software reaches production.

What has to be measurable

  • Detailed timesheet tracking logged against specific sprint user stories.
  • Weekly sprint velocity (story points completed versus planned).
  • Real-time budget burn reports against the monthly authorization cap.

T&M does not require baseline operational measurability (such as cost per file), which makes it easy to contract and dangerous to manage.

Where it fails

  1. The "Runaway Meter" dispute: The provider bills hundreds of hours investigating obscure legacy EDI quirks or bad data schemas without producing a working feature. Executive leadership cancels the contract in frustration.
  2. Lack of internal client product management: Without a decisive client product owner prioritizing the backlog, engineering teams build low-value features or drift into endless technical refactoring.

Buyer size and maturity

  • Best fit: Technology-mature logistics operators ($500M+ revenue) with experienced internal technical leadership who need specialized capacity augmentation.
  • Poor fit: Traditional non-technical mid-market 3PLs and freight forwarders who expect vendors to take full responsibility for business outcomes.

Does this structure pay for precondition work?

Yes, mechanically, but with severe client friction.

Under T&M, the provider can bill every hour spent manually reconciling duplicate carrier records or writing one-off tariff parsers.

However, clients suffer "precondition shock": - After six weeks and $120,000 in billings, the client asks to see the new automation interface. - The provider explains that all billable hours were consumed cleaning carrier tax IDs and mapping EDI schemas. - The buyer feels misled, believing they paid for software development rather than data entry.

T&M allows the provider to get paid for preconditions, but frequently burns commercial trust in the process.

Boutique positioning against Tier 1 SIs

Boutique firms use structured T&M to dismantle Tier 1 staffing pyramids:

  • Senior technical delivery vs junior billable armies: Tier 1 firms sell a "pyramid" model (one partner, one manager, and five junior offshore analysts logging 200 hours a week to learn the client's business). A boutique fields a "needle team" of two principal engineers who write production SQL and API code from day one.
  • Sprint-level termination rights: Boutiques offer no-fault sprint-level cancellation (client can stop at the end of any two-week sprint). This de-risks the T&M contract for the buyer compared to Tier 1 minimum commitments of six months.

Where this appears elsewhere

Systems implementing this include Blue Yonder (formerly JDA Software / Manugistics), Boutiques and specialist advisories, CargoWise (WiseTech Global), Cass Information Systems, Denim (formerly Axle Payments), Descartes Systems Group.
