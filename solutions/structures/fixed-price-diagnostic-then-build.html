In 90 seconds

What it is

A two-phase commercial engagement that uncouples operational and data discovery from technology delivery, establishing an audited baseline before scoping the implementation build

How it works mechanically

The engagement splits into two legally separate statements of work (SOW).

Phase 1 is a time-boxed, fixed-fee diagnostic (typically 3 to 4 weeks, priced between $35,000 and $60,000).

The provider audits transaction ledgers, profiles database tables, shadows operational desks, and maps exception queues.

Phase 1 produces three deliverables: 1. An empirical baseline of unit-of-work economics (e.g., cost per load, touches per shipment file, exception rates). 2. A data hygiene audit profiling master data errors (duplicate carrier records, missing tariff tables, HTS classification gaps). 3. A fixed-scope, fixed-price or capped-T&M proposal for Phase 2 (the build), with data cleansing explicitly scoped.

The client holds no obligation to proceed to Phase 2.

If the client proceeds, a portion of the diagnostic fee (e.g., 50%) is frequently credited against Phase 2 development milestones.

What it suits

  • Carrier and customer master data consolidation addressing fragmented carrier master after acquisition.
  • Freight audit and payment automation addressing billing exception leakage and purchased-transportation accrual failure.
  • Quote-to-book workflow automation addressing quote-to-book manual handoff.
  • Warehouse labour productivity instrumentation addressing contract vintage margin decay.

Risk allocation

  • Buyer risk: Low. The client commits minimal capital ($35k–$60k) to validate provider capability without committing to an open-ended multi-million dollar software project.
  • Provider risk: Low to Moderate. The provider avoids committing to a fixed-price build contract over dirty client data. The risk is limited to the provider failing to convert the diagnostic into a build contract.

What has to be measurable

Phase 1 requires read-only SQL access to production database replicas or comprehensive CSV exports: - Transaction volume counts (loads, orders, or shipment files). - Operational personnel headcount allocated to the workflow. - Historical dispute rates and accessorial fee ledgers.

If the buyer refuses database access and offers only executive interviews, the diagnostic cannot establish an empirical baseline.

Where it fails

  1. The RFP handoff: The buyer accepts the diagnostic report, acknowledges the findings, and uses the provider's architecture roadmap to issue a competitive tender to lower-cost commodity offshore developers.
  2. Slide-deck syndrome: The diagnostic delivers high-level strategy slides rather than data profiling and technical prototypes, alienating frontline operations leaders.

Buyer size and maturity

  • Best fit: Mid-market operators ($100M to $750M revenue) with legacy systems, high operational skepticism, and lack of internal enterprise architecture teams.
  • Poor fit: Early-stage venture brokerages (require immediate sprint capacity) and global mega-enterprises (procurement rules prohibit multi-phase sole-source awards).

Does this structure pay for precondition work?

Yes. This is the primary structure that solves the precondition trap.

As established in logistics automation preconditions are data and contract alignment, data hygiene and contract standardization represent the majority of implementation effort.

In a single monolithic build contract, buyers reject paying for data cleansing because it produces no visible features.

The diagnostic structure solves this by exposing the dirty data in Phase 1: - The provider proves that 40% of carrier records are duplicated or that 60% of tariff contracts sit in un-digitized PDFs. - The provider prices "Master Data Cleansing and Tariff Digitization" as Milestone 1 of the Phase 2 build. - The buyer approves the data work because Phase 1 proved the automation software cannot launch without it.

Boutique positioning against Tier 1 SIs

A boutique consulting firm can systematically out-maneuver Tier 1 systems integrators (Accenture, Big 4) on commercial structure:

  • Signing authority arbitrage: Tier 1 discovery engagements require $150,000 to $300,000 minimum fees to cover partner overhead, triggering formal corporate procurement, legal review, and multi-vendor tenders. A boutique prices Phase 1 at $45,000—deliberately sitting under typical VP signing limits ($50,000). The VP can sign the SOW on corporate credit card or discretionary budget in 48 hours without procurement interference.
  • Hands-on database profiling vs interview slides: Tier 1 firms staff diagnostics with junior analysts conducting stakeholder interviews. A boutique deploys a senior technical architect who extracts database schemas and runs SQL data-profiling scripts in week one, delivering concrete data proof that builds operational trust.

Where this appears elsewhere
