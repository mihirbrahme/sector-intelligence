The freight task

Indonesia's freight task is split between domestic inter-island distribution, resource extraction, and international container gateways.

Java dominates the domestic economy. It generates approximately 58% of national GDP and over 70% of manufacturing output.

This concentration causes severe directional freight imbalance. Outbound vessels carry manufactured goods, processed foods, and building materials from Java to Outer Islands.

Return vessels from Sumatra, Kalimantan, Sulawesi, and Papua face empty container rates between 60% and 80%. Backhaul cargo consists primarily of raw agricultural commodities.

This imbalance inflates round-trip shipping rates. Shipping a container from Jakarta to Jayapura in Papua often costs more than shipping from Jakarta to Rotterdam.

Total logistics expenditure historically absorbed 23% to 24% of national GDP. The government National Logistics Ecosystem (NLE) initiative targets reducing this to 14% to 15%.

Port of Tanjung Priok in Jakarta handles over 50% of containerized international trade. Secondary regional hubs include Tanjung Perak in Surabaya, Belawan in Medan, and Makassar in Sulawesi.

Tanjung Perak functions as the primary domestic maritime consolidation hub for Eastern Indonesia. Makassar acts as the transhipment gateway for eastern island trades.

Inland transport on Java relies heavily on road trucking. The 1,167-kilometre Trans-Java Toll Road connects Jakarta to Surabaya in under 20 hours.

Bulk resource flows bypass standard container networks. Thermal coal moves along the Mahakam River in East Kalimantan via tug and barge fleets.

Nickel ore moves by coastal barges between island mines and regional smelters in Central Sulawesi and North Maluku.

Pelindo operates under state directives to modernize national ports while extracting commercial terminal dividends.

The 2021 Pelindo merger concentrated terminal stevedoring tariffs under single corporate control. Port users face uniform rate increases.

Family-controlled shipping lines maintain deep political and regional distributor relationships. They protect fleet capital by securing multi-year captive cargo commitments.

Regulation

Strict cabotage policy

Law No. 17/2008 on Shipping and Presidential Instruction No. 5/2005 enforce absolute cabotage across Indonesian waters.

All vessels conducting domestic maritime transport must be Indonesian-flagged. Vessels must be owned by Indonesian entities with at least 51% Indonesian shareholding.

Vessels must be crewed entirely by Indonesian nationals. Foreign-flagged container ships cannot carry cargo between domestic Indonesian ports.

Foreign shipping lines are restricted to discharging import cargo at designated international gateway ports.

Port authority and concessions

The Ministry of Transportation acts as port regulator through local Port Authorities (Otoritas Pelabuhan / KSOP).

Pelindo holds commercial port concessions. Private industrial terminals (TUKS and Tersus) face strict legal restrictions against handling commercial third-party cargo.

Foreign ownership and licensing

Foreign ownership in domestic sea transport is legally capped at 49% under Law 17/2008.

The Job Creation Law (Omnibus Law) liberalized foreign equity in freight forwarding and warehousing. However, customs brokerage (PPJK) requires local licensing.

Customs and the National Logistics Ecosystem

Customs declarations run through the Directorate General of Customs and Excise Ceisa 4.0 system and the Indonesia National Single Window (INSW).

The government National Logistics Ecosystem (NLE) integrates customs clearance, port terminal gate appointments, and truck dispatch into a single platform.

Over-dimension and over-loading (ODOL)

The Ministry of Transportation has targeted the elimination of overloaded trucks. Full enforcement has been repeatedly delayed.

Shippers and fleet operators warn that strict ODOL enforcement would instantly reduce available road freight capacity by 30% to 50%.

Where the cost differs

Indonesia combines extremely low base labor costs with high inter-island transport and infrastructure expenses.

  • Labour: Minimum wages in Java range from RM equivalent $150 USD monthly in Central Java to $330 USD in Jakarta. Low wages make warehouse LMS automation difficult to justify economically.
  • Fuel: Subsidized diesel (Solar) is legally restricted for industrial transport. Trucks face fuel rationing, black-market markups, and long station queues.
  • Toll expenses: The Trans-Java Toll Road reduces transit time but imposes high tolls. Empty return trucks routinely divert to congested national roads (Pantura) to avoid tolls.
  • Inter-island shipping rates: Backhaul container repositioning adds 40% to 60% in deadhead voyage costs across eastern archipelago routes.
  • Metric impact: Revenue per employee in metric dictionary is depressed by large administrative and warehouse workforces. Fleet utilization is penalized by port waiting times.

Candidate missing mechanisms

  • Archipelagic directional deadhead penalty: Ships sail fully laden outbound from Java but return with empty containers, eroding voyage margins.
  • Informal transit extortion leakage ("pungli"): Unofficial cash payments at weighbridges, road checkpoints, and port gates leak outside accounting systems.
  • Port monopoly tariff imposition: Pelindo single-operator tariff increases raise landside drayage and stevedoring costs without market competition.

What is sold here

What buyers purchase

Large operators invest in compliance, telematics, and track-and-trace software. Mid-sized trucking fleets prioritize anti-theft and fuel-monitoring systems.

Key solution fits: - Event-driven visibility data pipeline: Tracks container locations across complex inter-island barge and ferry handoffs. - Document processing and customs entry automation: Automates INSW single window filings and Ceisa customs manifests. - Detention and demurrage dispute recovery: Audits container dwell and disputed demurrage fees at Tanjung Priok. - Freight audit and payment automation: Verifies complex inter-island multi-leg freight bills against contracted tariffs.

Harder preconditions

  • Warehouse labor management systems (LMS) face poor software ROI due to low local wages.
  • Informal road freight operators lack structured ERPs or electronic bank settlement.
  • Government-mandated single windows (NLE) reduce private willingness to purchase independent booking portals.
