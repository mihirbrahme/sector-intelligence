The freight task

CSCMP put United States business logistics costs at $2.58 trillion, 8.8% of GDP, for CY2024. Transportation was $1.68 trillion of that: trucking $985 billion, rail $102 billion, air $85 billion, water $52 billion. Inventory carrying cost was $720 billion. The row sits in benchmark register.

Two things about that split matter more than the totals.

Trucking is roughly ten times rail by spend and comparable by tonne-mile. Rail moves similar ton-miles for a tenth of the money because it carries bulk over long distances at low value density. A modal comparison using spend answers a different question from one using ton-miles, and neither is the question an operator asks.

The domestic long-haul market is genuinely long. A transcontinental truckload runs 2,000 to 2,800 miles. That distance is what makes truckload brokerage a standalone industry here and nowhere else in this record. There is enough linehaul cost inside a single load to support a margin between a shipper price and a carrier price.

Freight task Typical mode Why
Long-haul general merchandise Truckload, or intermodal above roughly 700 miles Distance amortises intermodal terminal time.
Multi-stop industrial and retail replenishment LTL over a fixed terminal network Shipments of 1,000 to 10,000 lb do not fill a trailer.
Grain, coal, chemicals, finished vehicles Class I rail Unit trains and captive origin points.
Petrochemicals and refined product, Gulf and Mississippi Inland tank barge Fifteen barges behind one towboat. No road or rail equivalent on cost per ton-mile.
Hawaii, Alaska, Guam, Puerto Rico Protected liner shipping Jones Act. The only lawful carriers are US-built, US-flagged, US-crewed.
Import containers, port to inland Drayage plus intermodal or transload Port-adjacent warehouse capacity decides which.
Iron ore, limestone, coal on the Lakes Great Lakes bulk Seasonal and lock-constrained. Shared with Canada.

Inland waterways run roughly 12,000 commercially navigable miles across 192 commercially active locks. Lock delay is a published external series — USACE LPMS, registered in benchmark register — and is the anchor for Kirby's delay days.

The operator landscape

Concentration differs by model far more than it differs by geography, and reading it wrong is the commonest error in this market.

Model Structure What that does to the analysis
Truckload (Model 4) Extremely fragmented. The FMCSA Company Census listed 2,204,341 active motor carriers at 2026-07-07; roughly 580,000 own or lease at least one tractor (June 2025), and ATA puts 91.5% of those at ten or fewer trucks. No carrier holds a material national share. No operator sets price. Rate is exogenous. Read cost per mile against a published rate, never market share. Use the 580,000 figure, not the census total — the census counts registrants, including private and non-operating entities.
LTL (Model 5) Highly concentrated. Yellow's 2023 liquidation removed a top-three national network; the survivors carry most of the national tonnage. FedEx Freight separated from FedEx on 2026-06-01 and now files its own 10-K as NYSE: FDXF. Terminal real estate is the barrier to entry, and it changed hands once, in 2023-24. Saia's +20.26% invested capital CAGR is that purchase. The FDXF spin gives the model a fifth pure-play filer; any peer distribution computed before June 2026 rests on segment data inside FDX.
Brokerage (Model 1) Fragmented at the tail, concentrated at the head. Tens of thousands of licensed brokers; a small number carry most of the enterprise gross revenue. Net revenue per load and loads per employee separate head from tail. Gross revenue does not.
Class I rail (Model 7 input) Six railroads operating as regional duopolies east and west. Purchased linehaul has no competitive substitute on most lanes. This is uncontrolled purchased input behaving as a fixed cost in its purest form.
Contract logistics (Model 3) Moderately concentrated among global 3PLs, with a long private tail. Contract form — open book, closed book, gainshare — decides the margin and is rarely disclosed.
Ports and terminals (Model 6) Public landlord authorities; private terminal operators on long leases. Terminal economics sit inside carrier and operator accounts, not in a port authority report.

Union status is a real cost discriminator inside Model 5, and comparing on it does not violate root, because both sides share the model. ABF Freight and TForce Freight are Teamsters-represented under a national master agreement. Old Dominion and Saia are not. Wage rate, work-rule flexibility and multiemployer pension exposure all follow, and all three land in the operating ratio.

Regulation

The US regulatory layer is why this geography produces verifiable evidence. Almost every rule below generates a public record against which a company's own account can be checked independently.

FMCSA — operating authority and safety fitness

Interstate for-hire motor carriers, brokers and freight forwarders register with the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration and hold a USDOT number and operating authority. Registration, safety and insurance records are public and searchable, which makes them externally generated — under root.

  • Financial responsibility. Minimum public liability for general freight is $750,000 per occurrence (49 CFR 387.9), unchanged in nominal terms since 1980. Hazardous materials classes run to $5,000,000. The statutory floor is now economically irrelevant. What matters is the self-insured retention and the excess tower above it.
  • Broker and freight forwarder security. $75,000, held as a BMC-84 surety bond or BMC-85 trust (49 CFR 387.307). Raised from $10,000 by MAP-21 in 2013, which removed several thousand small brokers from the register inside one compliance cycle.
  • Broker transparency is still proposed, not law. FMCSA issued a notice of proposed rulemaking in November 2024 that would rewrite 49 CFR 371.3 electronic transaction records, and a copy to a motor carrier or shipper within 48 hours of request, showing charges, payments, freight description, amounts, dates and claims. The Unified Agenda listed a supplemental notice for July 2026; it had not published as at mid-2026, and the proposal has since cleared the agency for interagency review. Section 371.3 stands as written and no new disclosure obligation attaches to brokers. If it lands, it changes manual carrier sourcing under rate volatility economics directly, because the buy rate stops being private.
  • Safety Measurement System. Roadside inspection and crash data scored into BASIC categories. A conditional or unsatisfactory safety rating, and an authority revocation, are public events with dates attached.
  • Drug and Alcohol Clearinghouse. The second Clearinghouse rule carries a compliance date of 2024-11-18. From that date state driver licensing agencies must downgrade the commercial driving privilege of a driver in prohibited status, and must query the Clearinghouse before issuing, renewing, upgrading or transferring a CDL or CLP. That converts a records system into a capacity-removal mechanism: the driver leaves the labour pool until the return-to-duty process is complete, rather than moving to another carrier.
  • English language proficiency. An April 2025 Executive Order directed enforcement of the existing ELP requirement. CVSA added English proficiency to the North American Standard Out-of-Service Criteria effective 2025-06-25, and FMCSA published an ELP out-of-service rule in the Federal Register on 2026-08-10. Enforcement is at roadside, not at hiring, so it removes drivers already dispatched.
  • Non-domiciled CDLs. An interim final rule published 2025-09-29 restricted non-domiciled CLP and CDL eligibility to holders of specified employment-based nonimmigrant status. The D.C. Circuit stayed the IFR pending review on 2025-11-13. A final rule was published 2026-02-13, effective 2026-03-16. Litigation is live and the interaction between the stay and If it holds, it removes a defined slice of the driver pool, which is a capacity event of the same class as the Clearinghouse downgrade.

What this gives the record. A disclosed carrier-pool reduction can be checked against the authority register independently of what the company says about it.

Hours of service and the ELD mandate

Property-carrying drivers may drive 11 hours inside a 14-hour on-duty window after 10 consecutive hours off duty, with a 30-minute break after 8 cumulative driving hours. Weekly limits are 60 hours in 7 days or 70 in 8, restartable after 34 consecutive hours off. Sleeper-berth time may be split 8/2 or 7/3. Adverse driving conditions extend the driving limit and the window by two hours. The short-haul exception releases a driver operating within 150 air-miles and returning inside 14 hours from the logging requirement.

Electronic logging devices have been mandatory under 49 CFR Part 395 subpart B since December 2019, with a narrow exemption for pre-2000 engines. Devices are vendor self-certified against a technical specification and listed on an FMCSA register. The agency removes non-compliant devices, which has happened repeatedly and strands the fleets running them.

What this does to the metrics. The binding capacity unit is the driver hour, not the mile. A carrier cannot buy back detention time with a faster truck. Detention therefore surfaces as a revenue-per-truck-per-week problem in Model 4 rather than a cost-per-mile problem, and that is the operational content behind detention and demurrage under-recovery on the road side.

Commercial auto liability and self-insured retention

This is the fastest-moving cost line in US road freight and the one least visible in a rate index.

  • ATRI's 2020 study of verdicts above $1 million found the average award in that cohort rose from $2,305,736 in 2010 to $22,288,000 in 2018 — a 967% increase, against 1.7% annual standard inflation over the same period. The count also moved: 26 cases above $1 million in the first five years of the data, close to 300 in the last five. independent research body, original data collection. The series has not been extended past 2018.
  • Carriers and brokers respond by retaining more risk at the bottom and buying excess above it. The retentions are disclosed, and are the readable part. Landstar carries a $10 million per-occurrence self-insured retention (2025-Q4). Knight-Swift held $602.8 million in total claims reserves at 2026-06-30. C.H. Robinson carries $155 million per occurrence over a $5 million deductible. All.
  • The consequence is a large annual cost with a volume-independent floor. In a trough it is one reason Model 4 operating ratios exceeded 100% across the peer set in FY2024 and FY2025 — see the Model 4 distribution in benchmark register.

Several states moved on tort procedure from 2024. Georgia's 2025 package narrowed the state's direct-action route against a motor carrier's insurer; Texas and Louisiana have legislated on bifurcation and on what evidence reaches a jury. Venue is therefore a live variable in the cost of the same accident.

Broker liability after Montgomery

The question was whether the Federal Aviation Administration Authorization Act (49 U.S.C. § 14501(c)) preempts a state-law negligent-selection claim against a freight broker, and whether the statute's safety exception saves it. The circuits split. The Ninth Circuit allowed the claim in Miller v. C.H. Robinson (2020); the Seventh Circuit barred it in Ye v. GlobalTranz (2023). That split is and durable.

The Supreme Court resolved it on 2026-05-14 in Montgomery v. Caribe Transport II, LLC (No. 24-1238), unanimously, Barrett J. writing. A state-law negligent hiring claim against a freight broker falls within the FAAAA's safety exception and is therefore not preempted. Requiring a broker to exercise ordinary care in selecting a carrier concerns the motor vehicles that end up on the road as a result. The Seventh Circuit was reversed.

Three details decide how far it reaches:

  • The broker defendant was C.H. Robinson, which is why the holding surfaces in its 2026-Q2 disclosure. the record's company file is a party record, not commentary.
  • The pleaded facts were a conditional FMCSA safety rating with documented deficiencies in driver qualification, hours of service, maintenance and crash rate. The evidence that establishes the claim is the public register — which

rather than on what a company says about its own vetting. - Intrastate is unresolved. The FAAAA subsection governing intrastate broker services carries no safety exception, and the Court expressly declined to decide whether preemption still bars negligent hiring claims on purely intrastate loads. That is a live question, not a settled one.

Lipe v. Lupus Superior — Dallas County jury verdict, 2026-07-23 — priced the exposure at $604 million on a 23% fault apportionment, reaching vicarious liability through a borrowed-employee finding. Under appeal. The full evidence chain, including the five-company response inside three quarters, is in carrier-selection liability repricing the cost of cheap capacity and in carrier qualification tightening compresses broker margin.

Why it belongs in a geography file. The exposure is jurisdictional, not commercial. A Canadian or UK broker tendering freight onto a US lane acquires it; the same broker moving domestic freight at home does not. See Canada.

FMC — ocean, detention and demurrage

The Federal Maritime Commission regulates ocean common carriers, marine terminal operators and ocean transportation intermediaries under the Shipping Act. Three things matter operationally.

  • Licensing. NVOCCs and ocean freight forwarders are licensed as ocean transportation intermediaries and bonded. An NVOCC must publish a tariff or operate under negotiated service arrangements.
  • The 2020 interpretive rule on demurrage and detention. A charge is assessed against whether it serves its incentive purpose — moving cargo. A charge levied while the terminal was closed, or the container unavailable, is challengeable on its own terms.
  • The 2024 billing rule (46 CFR Part 541), published 2024-02-26 and effective 2024-05-28. It requires 20 data elements on every demurrage or detention invoice — the 13 specified in the Ocean Shipping Reform Act plus 7 the Commission added. It sets a 30-day billing window: a VOCC or marine terminal operator must invoice within 30 calendar days of the charge last accruing, and an NVOCC within 30 days of the invoice it received being issued. The billed party then has 30 days to request mitigation and the biller 30 days to respond. An invoice missing any required element eliminates the obligation to pay that charge.One provision has been struck out and it is the one most often quoted. On 2025-09-23 the D.C. Circuit set aside 46 CFR 541.4, the section restricting who may be billed to the contracting party or the consignee. There is no longer a federal rule limiting who a demurrage invoice may be addressed to. The Commission may revisit it by further rulemaking with fuller justification. Everything else in Part 541 survives, including the invoice-content requirements and the 30-day deadline.

The surviving content requirement is why detention and demurrage under-recovery has a statutory recovery route in the US and only a contractual one elsewhere. It turns a demurrage dispute from a negotiation into a document test, which is what makes detention and demurrage dispute recovery saleable here on a recovery-fee contingency. A recovery argument built on the wrong-party ground is now dead; one built on missing invoice elements or a late invoice is not.

The rule reaches ocean carriers and marine terminal operators. It does not reach the truck side of a port. Chassis per-diem and dray detention are governed by contract and state law. Root applies, and it is satisfied: the absence of a federal truck-side remedy is documented in the rule's own scope, not inferred from a failed search.

STB — rail

The Surface Transportation Board regulates rail rates, service and consolidation under the ICC Termination Act of 1995.

  • Rate regulation applies only where the railroad is market dominant revenue over variable cost above 180%. Above the threshold a shipper may challenge under Stand-Alone Cost, Simplified-SAC, Three-Benchmark, or the Final Offer Rate Review procedure adopted in 2022 for smaller disputes. Below it, the rate is unreviewable.
  • Reciprocal switching for inadequate service. The Board adopted a service-standards-based rule in 2024, letting a shipper served by one railroad obtain switching to a second where the incumbent misses defined service metrics. It is a service remedy, not a rate remedy.
  • Service metrics reporting. Class I railroads file weekly performance data in Docket EP 770 — velocity, dwell, cars online, crew counts. external series and the only public operational data on a Model 7 operator's largest purchased input. Already carried in the monitoring plan, block 2.
  • Merger review runs under 49 CFR Part 1180, and Class I combinations must meet an enhanced competition standard adopted in 2001. The Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern combination is live before the Board: joint application filed 2025-12-19; rejected as incomplete 2026-01-16; revised application filed 2026-04-30; accepted as complete effective 2026-05-28 but held in abeyance pending supplemental information due 2026-07-27; the Board then adopted a procedural schedule and removed the proceeding, including environmental review, from abeyance. Union Pacific's own filings put expected completion in 2027. The outcome changes the eastern duopoly and with it every Model 7 cost base east of the Mississippi.
  • Revenue adequacy determinations are published annually and record the Board's own view of whether a railroad earns its cost of capital.

Drayage, ports and the state layer

Federal law is thin at the port gate. State law is not.

  • California AB5 (2019) applies the ABC test to worker classification. After the Ninth Circuit's 2021 decision in CTA v. Bonta and the denial of certiorari in 2022, it applies to trucking. California drayage has restructured toward employee models, two-check arrangements and business-to-business exemptions. It is the largest single cost-structure difference between a California drayage operation and a Texas one.
  • CARB Advanced Clean Fleets is not enforceable against drayage. ACF would have required zero-emission drayage registration from 2024-01-01. CARB withdrew the waiver request on 2025-01-13 and announced it would not enforce the portions requiring a federal waiver, including the high-priority and drayage fleet provisions. The state and local government fleet provisions need no waiver and remain enforced.
  • Three California waivers were revoked by Congress. Congressional Review Act resolutions covering Advanced Clean Trucks, the heavy-duty low-NOx Omnibus and Advanced Clean Cars II were signed into law on 2025-06-12. EPA sent four further California waivers to Congress in June 2026 for potential nullification. The practical effect: the California zero-emission truck mandate is not currently a cost driver for a drayage operator, and any provider case study predating 2025 that assumes it is, is stale. applies to those.
  • Clean Truck Fund rate at Los Angeles and Long Beach: $10 per TEU, $20 for containers longer than 20 feet, on loaded import and export containers, in force since 2022-04-01, with exemptions for low-NOx and zero-emission trucks. It is an accessorial, passed through, and belongs with port fees rather than linehaul cost.
  • Appointment systems and terminal fees. Terminals run appointment systems; PierPass OffPeak at San Pedro Bay carries a traffic mitigation fee. Both decide when a dray move can happen, which is what turns terminal density into delay berth, yard and landside constraints turn terminal density into delay.
  • Interstate administration. IFTA apportions fuel tax and IRP apportions registration across 48 states and the Canadian provinces; Unified Carrier Registration adds an annual per-fleet fee. The cost is small per vehicle and irreducible per carrier, so it falls hardest on the small end of a fragmented market.
  • Weights and dimensions. 80,000 lb gross on the Interstate system under the federal bridge formula. Twin 28-foot trailers are federally permitted; 33-foot pups are not, which caps LTL linehaul productivity nationally.

Where the cost differs

  • Driver wage and benefit is the largest single line in Models 4 and 5, and it is sticky downward. ATRI put the marginal cost of trucking at $2.336 per mile for CY2025 and $1.854 excluding fuel — the highest in the series and 3.4% above CY2024. The row is in benchmark register.
  • Insurance is the fastest-growing line, and it has a volume-independent floor. See the commercial auto section.
  • Fuel is passed through by convention, pegged to the EIA weekly retail on-highway diesel series with a base price and a cents-per-mile step. The peg is contractual and the reset periodic, which is exactly where escalator and surcharge recovery lag under input inflation lives.
  • Empty miles ran 13.5% to 16.0% in FY2025 against 10.5% to 12.0% at the FY2021 peak. Every empty mile carries full cost per mile and no revenue.
  • Terminal real estate is the Model 5 barrier. Owned, long-lived, and effectively unbuildable in the metros that matter. Yellow's liquidation was the only material addition to national terminal supply in a decade.
  • Chassis are a separate rental market in Model 7. Chassis per-diem and split chassis pools are a US-specific accessorial that non-US intermodal benchmarks do not carry.

What this does to the metrics. Operating ratio is the right frame inside Models 4 and 5 and meaningless across them. Cost per mile is right for Model 4 and wrong for Model 5, where cost per shipment and revenue per hundredweight are the unit. For Model 1 it is net revenue per load, per root.

Candidate missing mechanisms:

  • Insurance retention rising faster than the rate base. The financial signature is claims and insurance expense per mile growing while revenue per mile is flat or falling, with the retention step disclosed separately from premium. Distinct from carrier-selection liability repricing the cost of cheap capacity, which is about pool access rather than retained cost.
  • Chassis pool fragmentation converting a variable rental into an unavoidable accessorial. The signature is per-diem cost per container turn rising while turns per box fall.
  • State classification divergence stranding an owner-operator model. The signature is a step change in driver cost in one state's operations with no change in miles, wage rate or fleet size.

What is sold here

Most of what sits in 05-solutions/ was built and sold here. Two things make the US the easiest market to sell into and one makes it the hardest.

Easiest, because the data exists. An open rate series, a public carrier register and quarterly segment disclosure mean a business case can be built from outside the company and checked afterwards. And because exception cost is quantified: clean invoices clear at $0.50 to $1.50, exceptions cost $10.00 to $25.00 in coordinator labour.

Hardest, because the buyer has bought before. Every pattern below has a failed predecessor in 05-solutions/failure history/, and a US operator has usually seen one.

Patterns that sell:

  • Carrier onboarding and compliance automation the direct response to carrier-selection liability repricing the cost of cheap capacity. The constraint is vetting throughput, not vetting standard.
  • Freight audit and payment automation 1.0% to 3.5% net recovery on gross freight spend, pre-payment.
  • Detention and demurrage dispute recovery the FMC billing rule gives it a documented statutory basis.
  • Carrier settlement and purchased transportation payment with transport finance and working capital quick-pay economics are a US convention.
  • Dynamic pricing and buy-rate guidance this works only where a published buy-side series exists.
  • Exception triage and resolution workflow.

Providers concentrated here: McLeod, MercuryGate, Revenova, Turvo and Trimble TMW in TMS; Cass, TriumphPay, HubTran and Denim in settlement and audit; project44 in visibility; rate and market data providers for the series everything else is priced against.
