Every measure used on this site, with the model it belongs to. A metric read against the wrong model is worse than no metric, because it looks like an answer.

Net Revenue / Adjusted Gross Profit (brokerage)

  • Definition: gross revenue less purchased transportation and related services
  • Why it matters: the only revenue line for a broker that reflects company performance rather than market rates
  • Moves up: buying capacity below the price sold; contract mix over spot in a falling market; spot exposure in a rising market
  • Moves down: manual load coverage forcing market-rate buys; customer mix shifting to low-margin contract freight
  • Where to find it: 10-K MD&A, usually a named non-GAAP reconciliation table. Often absent from standard XBRL taxonomy — extract from filing text.
  • Reference: C.H. Robinson FY2025: $2,729,410k, 16.8% of total revenue (10-K filed 2026-02-13)
  • Common misread: comparing across companies with different contract/spot mix
  • Diagnostic question: When a tender comes in at a rate below what you can buy that lane for today, who makes the cover-or-decline call, and what do they look at before they make it?

Adjusted Operating Margin (brokerage)

  • Definition: operating income ÷ adjusted gross profit
  • Why it matters: the company's own disclosed efficiency measure. Using it signals you read the filing rather than a screener.
  • Moves up: headcount productivity gains; automation of load coverage
  • Moves down: personnel cost growth outpacing net revenue; acquisition integration overhead
  • Reference: C.H. Robinson FY2025: 29.1% (operating income $794,961k ÷ adjusted gross profit $2,729,410k)
  • Common misread: confusing with GAAP operating margin on gross revenue (4.9% for the same company, same year)
  • Diagnostic question: How many people touch a single load between the customer tender and the carrier invoice being paid, and what does each of them do to it?

Net Revenue per Load

  • Definition: net revenue ÷ loads handled
  • Why it matters: the cleanest single measure of pricing discipline
  • Moves up: dynamic pricing; better carrier sourcing; freight mix to harder-to-cover lanes
  • Moves down: manual sourcing at market rates; commoditised lane mix
  • Where to find it: load counts sometimes disclosed in MD&A or investor decks; otherwise modelled
  • Common misread: ignoring lane mix. A falling number may be mix, not execution.
  • Diagnostic question: Which lanes did your reps earn the most on last quarter, and how did they know to price those lanes that way?

Loads per Rep per Day

  • Definition: loads handled ÷ (customer-facing headcount × working days)
  • Why it matters: the productivity metric brokers manage to internally. Using it signals operational fluency.
  • Moves up: automation of quoting, tendering, tracking, and check calls
  • Moves down: manual exception handling; fragmented systems post-acquisition
  • Where to find it: rarely disclosed. Model from headcount and load counts, label.
  • Diagnostic question: What does a rep actually do between 7am and 9am, and which of those steps would disappear if your systems talked to each other?

SG&A as % of Net Revenue (brokerage)

  • Definition: SG&A ÷ net revenue
  • Why it matters: for an asset-light business this is the cost structure
  • Moves up: headcount growth ahead of volume; integration overhead; duplicate systems
  • Moves down: automation; consolidation of acquired back offices
  • Common misread: computing against gross revenue, which makes every broker look efficient
  • Diagnostic question: Of the back offices you have acquired, how many are still running their own process on their own system, and what has kept them there?

Operating Ratio (asset carriage)

  • Definition: operating expenses ÷ revenue. Often quoted ex-fuel-surcharge.
  • Why it matters: the standard measure for asset carriers. Lower is better.
  • Moves up (worse): driver wage inflation; utilisation decline; insurance; empty miles
  • Moves down (better): density; utilisation; fuel efficiency
  • Common misread: comparing OR across carriers without normalising for fuel surcharge treatment and length of haul
  • Diagnostic question: Where do your empty miles come from - which lanes, which customers, and which planning decisions put them there?

Revenue per Truck per Week (dedicated and truckload carriage)

  • Definition: total freight revenue (commonly net of fuel surcharge, or gross) ÷ average operating tractors ÷ weeks in period
  • Why it matters: the definitive productivity and asset yield metric for motor carriers. In dedicated carriage, it reflects the contractual two-part tariff (fixed weekly truck charge + variable mileage rate).
  • Moves up: contractual minimum truck charge increases; higher weekly dispatched mileage per tractor; indexed rate escalations; accessorial capture
  • Moves down: unseated tractors; shipper volume curtailment below contracted minimums; unbilled equipment dwell; escalator recovery lag
  • Where to find it: disclosed in SEC Form 10-K and 10-Q MD&A tables (Werner, Schneider, Covenant, Marten, J.B. Hunt)
  • Reference: Werner Dedicated FY2025: $4,450–$4,800/wk net of fuel ($4,789 in Q2 2026); Schneider Dedicated FY2025: $4,004–$4,026/wk net of fuel; Covenant Dedicated FY2025: $4,453/wk; Marten Dedicated FY2024: $3,767/wk.
  • Common misread: comparing gross revenue per truck against net-of-fuel figures, or comparing dedicated yield against irregular-route OTR. OTR moves with spot linehaul rates; dedicated reflects fixed asset recovery.
  • Diagnostic question: What percentage of your revenue per truck per week is guaranteed under fixed weekly equipment charges versus variable mileage dispatches?

Fleet Utilisation against Committed Capacity (dedicated contract carriage)

  • Definition: actively dispatched and revenue-generating tractors ÷ total contractually committed fleet size on a dedicated customer account
  • Why it matters: dedicated accounts commit dedicated iron and drivers to a shipper. If shipper volume drops and tractors sit idle without minimum revenue guarantees, asset productivity collapses.
  • Moves up: consistent shipper dispatch cadence; efficient slip-seating; rapid seated-driver replacement; enforceable minimum volume commitments (MVCs)
  • Moves down: customer plant shutdowns or retail inventory cuts; localized driver recruitment deficits leaving trucks unseated; maintenance dwell
  • Where to find it: internal TMS dispatch platforms (Trimble TMW, McLeod LoadMaster); referenced qualitatively in carrier 10-K fleet disclosures
  • Common misread: evaluating dedicated utilization on miles per tractor per week. A dedicated store replenishment fleet with short hauls can show low weekly mileage but 100% committed asset utilization and high return on capital.
  • Diagnostic question: When a dedicated customer's dispatch volume drops 20% below forecast, how many tractors sit unseated, and does the contract enforce billing for the idle capacity?

Driver Turnover (dedicated versus OTR)

  • Definition: annualized driver separations (voluntary and involuntary) ÷ average active driver headcount
  • Why it matters: driver recruitment and onboarding costs $5,000 to $10,000+ per seat. Dedicated fleets act as a structural retention mechanism, insulating carriers from irregular-route driver churn.
  • Moves up (worse): unpredictable OTR schedules; extended time away from home; local industrial wage competition; shipper detention
  • Moves down (better): regular dedicated routes; predictable daily or weekly home time; guaranteed minimum weekly pay; newer equipment
  • Where to find it: American Trucking Associations (ATA) quarterly driver turnover surveys; carrier annual reports and ESG disclosures
  • Reference: Irregular-route OTR turnover runs at 80% to 95%+ (ATA large truckload carrier average); Dedicated contract fleets run at 30% to 45%; Private fleets run at 15% to 25%.
  • Common misread: treating 90% turnover as an exodus from the industry. It is primarily inter-carrier churn chasing sign-on bonuses. Dedicated routes eliminate the churn trigger by providing predictable home time.
  • Diagnostic question: What is the turnover differential between your dedicated accounts and your irregular-route OTR fleet, and what is that gap costing in recruiting overhead and unseated equipment?

Days Sales Outstanding (all logistics models)

  • Definition: receivables ÷ revenue × 365
  • Why it matters: brokers pay carriers fast and collect from shippers slowly. The gap is why freight factoring exists. A widening gap is a cash mechanism.
  • Moves up: customer mix to large shippers with long terms; billing exceptions and disputes; invoice error rates
  • Moves down: invoice accuracy; automated freight audit; collections discipline
  • Where to find it: balance sheet and income statement, computable for any filer
  • Common misread: treating it as a finance problem. It is usually a billing data quality problem.
  • Diagnostic question: Of the invoices you send in a week, how many come back queried, and what are the top three things they are queried about?

Gross Profit / Net Revenue (forwarding)

  • Definition: gross revenue less the cost of carrier space, freight, and directly attributable third-party charges
  • Why it matters: the only revenue line for a forwarder that reflects company performance. Gross revenue moves with ocean and air rate cycles, which the forwarder neither sets nor controls.
  • Moves up: buying space below the price sold; yield discipline on contracted allocations; mix toward customs brokerage and value-added services; spot exposure in a rising rate market
  • Moves down: volume bought at market on uncontracted space; mix toward low-yield FCL port-to-port; loss of allocation forcing spot buys
  • Where to find it: disclosed as "net revenue" or "gross profit" in forwarder reporting; European filers commonly present it as gross profit on the face of the income statement
  • Common misread: reading gross revenue growth as performance. A freight rate spike inflates gross revenue and can leave gross profit flat or lower.
  • Diagnostic question: The last time ocean rates moved sharply, how long did it take to reprice your spot customers, and who did that repricing?

Gross Profit per Shipment File

  • Definition: gross profit ÷ shipment files handled
  • Why it matters: the cleanest measure of yield per unit of work, and it is the denominator that matches the labour the file consumes. Cost is driven by files; revenue is not.
  • Moves up: mix toward air, project, and customs-heavy files; consolidation of small shipments into fewer, larger files; value-added services attached to the same file
  • Moves down: fragmentation of volume into more, smaller files; mix toward commoditised FCL; customer-driven splitting of bookings
  • Where to find it: file counts rarely disclosed. Model from headcount and volume disclosures, label.
  • Common misread: treating a fall as a pricing failure when it is file fragmentation. Yield per TEU can hold while yield per file collapses.
  • Diagnostic question: Your largest customer by volume - how many separate files does that volume arrive as, and who decided it should be that many?

Files per Operations FTE per Month

  • Definition: shipment files handled ÷ operations headcount ÷ months
  • Why it matters: forwarding cost is document-handling labour. This is the productivity number the operating model actually turns on.
  • Moves up: carrier and customs EDI coverage; automated document capture and classification; standing routing instructions removing per-file decisions
  • Moves down: manual rekeying between customer, carrier and customs systems; exception-heavy trade lanes; multiple TMS instances after acquisition
  • Where to find it: rarely disclosed. Model from segment headcount and volume, label.
  • Common misread: comparing across forwarders with different trade lane and customs mix. A customs-heavy file is not the same unit of work as a port-to-port FCL file.
  • Diagnostic question: For a routine file on your busiest trade lane, which data is keyed by a person and which arrives by integration?

Touches per File

  • Definition: discrete manual interventions required to move one shipment file from booking to invoice
  • Why it matters: it is the mechanism-level metric behind files per FTE. It localises where the labour goes, which is what a diagnosis needs.
  • Moves up: data arriving by email and PDF rather than integration; document discrepancies between booking, bill of lading and customs entry; manual rate lookup and quotation
  • Moves down: customer and carrier integration; document OCR with validation at capture; straight-through processing for compliant files
  • Where to find it: never disclosed. Established from process observation or interview; treat any figure as an estimate and say so.
  • Common misread: counting system steps rather than human interventions. A workflow with twenty automated steps and two touches is a good file.
  • Diagnostic question: On the last file that went wrong, which document disagreed with which, and at what point in the process did someone notice?

Units per Labour Hour (contract logistics)

  • Definition: units, cases, or order lines picked ÷ direct labour hours
  • Why it matters: in a labour-dominant cost structure this is the operating model's core productivity measure, and on open-book contracts it is usually the contracted commitment being measured against.
  • Moves up: slotting to demand profile; pick path optimisation; automation and goods-to-person; stable, trained labour
  • Moves down: agency labour and churn; profile shift to smaller, more frequent orders; peak staffing carried into non-peak volume
  • Where to find it: site-level operational data, not filings. Occasionally referenced in investor materials as a productivity improvement claim.
  • Common misread: comparing across sites with different order profiles. An each-pick e-commerce site and a full-pallet retail site are not comparable on this metric at any level.
  • Diagnostic question: At your worst-performing site, what does the shift supervisor say the pickers spend their time waiting on?

Cost per Order Line

  • Definition: total site cost ÷ order lines shipped
  • Why it matters: it is the unit economic the contract is priced on, and the number a client benchmarks at renewal. Margin lives in the gap between contracted rate per line and actual cost per line.
  • Moves up: wage inflation inside a fixed-price contract term; volume below the profile the site was designed and staffed for; rework from inventory accuracy failures
  • Moves down: labour productivity; automation with payback inside contract term; volume above design point absorbing fixed facility cost
  • Where to find it: not disclosed. Model from segment revenue and volume where available, label.
  • Common misread: reading a rising figure as execution failure when it is volume decline against a fixed cost base. Diagnose the denominator first.
  • Diagnostic question: Which of your sites was designed for volume that has not arrived, and what has changed about that customer since you signed?

Contract Renewal Rate (contract logistics and dedicated carriage)

  • Definition: contracted revenue or tractor commitment retained at renewal ÷ contract value up for renewal in the period
  • Why it matters: both contract logistics (warehousing) and dedicated contract carriage run on books of multi-year agreements (typically 3 to 5 years). Renewal is where accumulated inflation and service performance are repriced, and unrecovered capital (racking, custom trailers, daycabs) becomes an exposure if lost.
  • Moves up: high on-time delivery and service level compliance; deep systems integration (EDI, TMS, private yard dispatch); mutual operational dependency
  • Moves down: service failures inside the term; shipper insourcing (converting to private fleet) or switching to irregular-route 3PLs; cost drift above market
  • Where to find it: disclosed in investor presentations and annual reports (J.B. Hunt DCS discloses 95–98% renewal; Schneider, Werner, GXO)
  • Benchmark: 90% to 98% in mature dedicated contract carriage and contract logistics accounts.
  • Common misread: treating a high renewal rate as pure strength. Contracts renewed at concessions (conceding rate freezes or absorbing higher equipment costs to protect volume) count as retained while destroying account margin.
  • Diagnostic question: Of the dedicated accounts or warehouse contracts you renewed last year, which accepted lower rates or capped escalators, and what did the shipper point to when demanding the concession?

Revenue per Square Foot (contract logistics)

  • Definition: site or segment revenue ÷ operated square footage
  • Why it matters: facility cost is the second cost line after labour, and under IFRS 16 / ASC 842 the lease sits on the balance sheet for the full term whether or not the space is productive.
  • Moves up: mix toward value-added services and e-commerce fulfilment; higher throughput density; cube utilisation and automation
  • Moves down: contracted space held ahead of volume; storage-heavy, low-throughput contracts; a lost contract inside a lease term
  • Where to find it: square footage commonly disclosed in annual reports and investor decks; revenue at segment level
  • Common misread: treating it as a pricing metric. It is mostly a mix and utilisation metric — a storage contract and a fulfilment contract have structurally different values with no difference in performance.
  • Diagnostic question: How much of the space you are paying for today has no contracted volume behind it, and how did it come to be empty?

Revenue per Hundredweight (LTL)

  • Definition: revenue ÷ (weight hauled in hundredweight), usually quoted both including and excluding fuel surcharge
  • Why it matters: the yield measure the whole LTL model is priced on. LTL sells space and weight through a terminal network, so revenue per hundredweight is where pricing discipline, class mix and freight profile all land in one number.
  • Moves up: general rate increases holding in contract renewals; mix toward higher-class, lower-density freight; accessorial capture; shedding underpriced tonnage
  • Moves down: weight per shipment rising on unchanged pricing; mix toward dense, low-class freight; discount creep on large national accounts; uncollected accessorials
  • Where to find it: disclosed by most public LTL carriers in MD&A and investor supplements, ex-fuel and including fuel
  • Common misread: reading a fall as a pricing failure when it is freight profile. Heavier, denser shipments mechanically lower revenue per hundredweight while raising revenue per shipment.
  • Diagnostic question: On your largest national account, when the freight profile changed, how long was it before pricing knew and who told them?

Weight per Shipment (LTL)

  • Definition: total weight hauled ÷ shipment count, in pounds
  • Why it matters: it is the denominator behind revenue per hundredweight and the link between rating accuracy and yield. Class and weight are what the invoice is built from, so errors here become billing disputes rather than pricing decisions.
  • Moves up: economic mix toward industrial and retail replenishment; customers consolidating orders; truckload spillover into the LTL network
  • Moves down: e-commerce and parcel-adjacent freight; customers splitting orders to hit service windows
  • Where to find it: disclosed in operating statistics tables alongside shipment and tonnage counts
  • Common misread: treating it as a demand signal only. A gap between booked weight and reweighed weight is a data quality mechanism, and it shows up as revenue adjustments, not as tonnage.
  • Diagnostic question: What share of shipments get reweighed or reclassified after pickup, and where in the process does the corrected number first appear?

Shipments per Terminal per Day (LTL)

  • Definition: shipments handled ÷ terminal count ÷ working days
  • Why it matters: network density is the LTL moat, and this is the measure of it. Terminal cost is largely fixed against door count, so density is what converts tonnage into operating ratio.
  • Moves up: volume growth into existing doors; lane rationalisation removing rehandles; pickup and delivery route density
  • Moves down: terminal openings ahead of the volume that pays for them; volume decline against an unchanged door base; overlapping coverage after acquisition
  • Where to find it: terminal and door counts disclosed in annual reports; shipment counts in operating statistics. Combine and label where either is period-averaged.
  • Common misread: comparing across carriers with different network shapes. A regional next-day carrier and a national long-haul carrier carry structurally different shipments per terminal at identical performance.
  • Diagnostic question: Which terminals opened in the last three years, and what volume did the plan assume they would be handling by now?

Operating Ratio (LTL)

  • Definition: total operating expenses ÷ total operating revenue, expressed as a percentage. Commonly evaluated ex-fuel-surcharge.
  • Why it matters: the core profitability metric of an LTL network. Because LTL runs high fixed terminal and pickup/delivery route costs, small volume shifts create dramatic OR swings through operating leverage.
  • Moves up (worse): terminal door additions ahead of freight volume; tonnage decline during freight recessions; wage and driver benefit inflation; rising cargo claims and self-insurance reserves.
  • Moves down (better): freight density absorbing fixed terminal overhead; consistent General Rate Increases (GRI); automated dimensioning and re-weighing capturing unbilled yield; optimal linehaul load factor.
  • Where to find it: primary headline metric in MD&A and earnings releases of public LTL carriers (e.g. Old Dominion, Saia, XPO).
  • Reference: Old Dominion Freight Line FY2025 OR: 73.8% (benchmark); peer group averages typically 88%–94%.
  • Common misread: comparing LTL OR directly to truckload (TL) carriers. LTL requires 20–30% gross margin to cover terminal network capex, whereas truckload requires far lower terminal reinvestment.
  • Diagnostic question: What percentage of your door capacity is currently utilised, and how much tonnage must drop before your linehaul routes lose break-even density?

Crane Moves per Hour (ports and terminals)

  • Definition: container moves ÷ crane working hours, measured gross (including all delays) or net (excluding them)
  • Why it matters: berth productivity determines vessel turnaround, and vessel turnaround is what the liner customer contracts for. It is the operating metric that connects quay capital to service performance.
  • Moves up: yard planning that presents boxes in stow order; twin-lift and tandem operation; stable labour and gang availability; equipment uptime
  • Moves down: yard congestion forcing rehandles; stowage plans arriving late or changed; internal transfer vehicle shortages; crane maintenance backlog
  • Where to find it: terminal operating data and liner service level reporting, not filings. Occasionally cited in concession authority statistics and investor materials.
  • Common misread: comparing gross and net rates across terminals as if they were the same measure. The delay definitions differ, and the gap between gross and net is itself the diagnostic.
  • Diagnostic question: On your worst berth window last quarter, what were the cranes waiting on, and who was tracking that while it happened?

Container Dwell Time (ports and terminals)

  • Definition: average elapsed time an import or export container occupies

  • Why it matters: dwell sets yard utilisation, and yard utilisation sets crane productivity through rehandles. It is also the revenue mechanism behind storage and demurrage, which makes it the one metric where operational failure and revenue point in opposite directions.

  • Moves up: customs and inspection holds; documentation not released before arrival; hinterland trucking or rail capacity shortfall; consignee using the yard as storage

  • Moves down: pre-arrival clearance and appointment systems; free-time

  • Where to find it: terminal operating systems and port authority statistics. Rarely in filings; storage and demurrage revenue is the visible proxy and an imperfect one.

  • Common misread: reading rising storage and demurrage revenue as commercial strength. It is usually a landside failure being billed, and it is the charge most often disputed and least often collected in full.
  • Diagnostic question: Of the boxes sitting past free time today, how many are held by customs, how many by paperwork, and how many because the consignee has nowhere to put them?

Container Turns per Year (intermodal)

  • Definition: total intermodal loads handled ÷ average active domestic container fleet size over a 12-month period.
  • Why it matters: the master capital efficiency metric for intermodal box fleets. It measures how many times per year an expensive capital asset generates revenue.
  • Moves up: fast rail ramp turnarounds; rapid consignee unloading and gate-out; balanced bi-directional corridor demand; scheduled appointment compliance.
  • Moves down: rail ramp congestion and terminal dwell; shipper and consignee detention using containers as temporary storage; corridor volume imbalances forcing empty staging.
  • Where to find it: calculate from SEC 10-K disclosures: segment loads ÷ average trailing container count. Label.
  • Reference: J.B. Hunt Intermodal (JBI) FY2025: ~17.5 turns/year (2.05M loads ÷ ~117k containers). Balanced market target: 20–22 turns/year; freight recession trough: 14–16 turns/year.
  • Common misread: assuming low turns always mean operational incompetence. Severe rail linehaul service failures or customer-side detention directly trap containers outside the intermodal operator's control.
  • Diagnostic question: How many days does an average container spend sitting on the street with a customer versus moving on a train or sitting on a rail ramp?

Dray Cost per Load (intermodal)

  • Definition: total drayage expense (origin pickup dray + destination delivery dray) ÷ total intermodal loads moved.
  • Why it matters: drayage represents 20–30% of total intermodal operating costs. It is the largest controllable operational variable in an intermodal movement.
  • Moves up: driver detention at shipper loading docks or congested rail ramps; long-distance drayage beyond 50–75 mile ramp catchments; reliance on spot third-party dray brokers; chassis split charges.
  • Moves down: high density of pickups within 30 miles of ramps; company-owned dray tractor fleets with high driver retention; drop-and-hook customer programs; automated gate systems (AGS).
  • Where to find it: rarely disclosed as a line item. Estimated from purchased transportation notes, tractor fleet headcount, and dray miles per load.
  • Common misread: assuming owned drayage is always cheaper than purchased drayage. In unbalanced or seasonal lanes, owned dray fleets suffer catastrophic idle driver and tractor expense.
  • Diagnostic question: What proportion of your dray moves involve an empty chassis split or wait longer than 90 minutes at the rail ramp gate?

Street and Ramp Dwell (intermodal)

  • Definition: elapsed time a domestic container spends either staged inside a railroad intermodal yard (ramp dwell) or held at a customer facility before return (street dwell), measured in days or hours.
  • Why it matters: dwell directly caps container velocity and turn rates. Ramp dwell triggers Class I rail storage fees, while street dwell risks unbilled customer equipment absorption.
  • Moves up: delayed consignee unloading; missed dray appointments; weekend rail ramp cut-off mismatches; customer warehouse receiving backlogs.
  • Moves down: strict demurrage/per diem billing enforcement; pre-cleared appointment scheduling; drop-trailer programs with strict free-time limits.
  • Where to find it: rail operational scorecards (STB weekly service metrics) for terminal dwell; carrier internal telematics (solar GPS container tracking).
  • Common misread: viewing customer detention charges as profitable revenue. Equipment per diem rarely compensates for the lost revenue of an un-turned box during peak demand.
  • Diagnostic question: How many containers in your fleet have exceeded 5 days of customer street dwell, and what percentage of that detention was invoiced and collected?

Empty Repositioning Share (intermodal)

  • Definition: total empty container miles (or empty railcar moves) ÷ total intermodal container miles (or moves), expressed as a percentage.
  • Why it matters: freight networks are structurally directional. Moving an empty container generates rail linehaul and drayage cash cost with zero offsetting revenue.
  • Moves up: headhaul/backhaul freight demand imbalance (e.g. Southern California import corridor vs. Midwest consumption); seasonal agricultural demand shifts.
  • Moves down: backhaul freight discounts; street-turning import boxes directly into domestic export loads; dynamic pricing balancing lane flows.
  • Where to find it: proprietary carrier telematics. Industry norms synthesised from STB freight data and intermodal market surveys.
  • Reference: Typically 15–25% across national intermodal networks.
  • Common misread: treating empty repositioning as avoidable waste. Running zero empty miles means refusing high-margin headhaul freight in major origin markets.
  • Diagnostic question: How much margin does your highest-yielding headhaul lane surrender to the cost of deadheading the empty box back?

Delay Days (inland marine)

  • Definition: lost time incurred by a tow — one towboat plus one or more barges — while stopped in transit. Kirby Corporation's FY2025 10-K scopes the causes explicitly: "weather, lock conditions, or other navigational factors."
  • Why it matters: the only published operational availability measure in inland marine, and the denominator of any productivity argument in model 8.
  • The definition is the trap. The disclosed causes are all external. Yard scheduling, crew availability, work bundling and maintenance sequencing are not in this number. A proposition to "split delay days into controllable and uncontrollable" is asking a metric to carry a component its own definition excludes. See a disclosed metric definition can exclude the component a value case rests on.
  • Moves up: fog and high wind on the Gulf Coast; high or low water on the river system; lock outages and chamber closures; ice.
  • Moves down: favourable seasonal weather; lock reliability; routing away from constrained chambers.
  • Where to find it: disclosed annually in Kirby Corporation's 10-K MD&A. No other operator located publishes it.
  • Correct denominator: tows, not barges. Express as delay days per average towboat-year. Kirby Corporation FY2025: 11,410 delay days over an average 279 inland towboats = 40.9 days per towboat-year, 11.2% of calendar. Dividing by the 1,105-barge count instead gives 10.3 and understates the metric by a factor of four.
  • Reference: Kirby Corporation 11,410 (2025), 11,583 (2024) — a 1% improvement. Grade ``.
  • External corroboration exists, unusually. USACE's Lock Performance Monitoring System publishes lock-level delay and closure data for 192 commercially active locks. company disclosure, and it can bound the lock component from outside. See benchmark register.
  • Common misread: reading a 1% annual improvement as management performance. In a metric whose disclosed drivers are all weather and infrastructure, a 1% move is within the noise of a mild winter.
  • Diagnostic question: "Of last year's delay days, how many chambers and how many weather events do they come from, and which of those did you route around?"

Barge Utilisation (inland marine)

  • Definition: the operator's own measure of the share of the tank barge fleet actively employed. Definitions are not standardised and no operator located publishes the calculation.
  • Why it matters: the demand read for model 8. Because fleet supply is approximately fixed industry-wide, utilisation moves with volumes, not with capacity decisions.
  • Moves up: petrochemical and refinery production volumes; refinery utilisation; export demand; barges out of service industry-wide.
  • Moves down: lighter feedstock mix; refinery outages and closures; new barge deliveries; cargo shifting to pipeline.
  • Where to find it: disclosed as a range, by quarter, in Kirby Corporation's 10-K and earnings releases. Never as a number.
  • Reference: Kirby Corporation inland 2025: low-to-mid 90% range (Q1, Q2), mid-80% range (Q3), mid-to-high 80% range (Q4); coastal mid-to-high 90% range through 2025 and 2024. Grade ``.
  • Common misread: quoting a full-year utilisation figure. Kirby Corporation's FY2025 10-K carries two different full-year characterisations of the same number in two places — "in the 90% range" and "decreased to the high 80% range" which is why the quarterly series is the one to use. Recorded as a source conflict in Kirby Corporation's evidence log rather than averaged.
  • Diagnostic question: "What is in the numerator — barges on hire, barges loaded, or barges not in the yard?"

Time-Charter Share of Term Revenue (marine)

  • Definition: the proportion of term-contract revenue billed on a daily vessel rate rather than per movement.
  • Why it matters: it determines whether delay is a revenue loss or a cost event, and therefore what a recovered day of availability is worth. This is the single most important disclosed mix figure in model 8 and the one most often skipped.
  • Moves up: customer preference for guaranteed capacity; the operator trading spot upside for revenue certainty.
  • Moves down: a tightening market in which the operator wants spot exposure.
  • Where to find it: Kirby Corporation discloses it directly in 10-K MD&A, separately for inland and coastal.
  • Reference: Kirby Corporation inland time charters as a share of inland term revenue: 63% (2023), 61% (2024), 59% (2025). Coastal approximately 90%, 98%, 100% of coastal term revenue over the same years, with coastal 100% term from 2025. Grade ``.
  • Derived: delay-exposed revenue = inland revenue x (1 − term share x time charter share). Kirby Corporation FY2025: 1 − (0.70 x 0.59) = 58.7% of inland revenue exposed; coastal effectively nil. Approximately 47% of marine segment revenue.
  • Common misread: treating "70% term" as the insulation figure. Term and time charter are different things — a term contract of affreightment carries full delay exposure at a fixed rate, which is the worst of both.
  • Diagnostic question: "On a day the tow does not move, which contracts still bill?"

Escalator Capture and Reset Lag

  • Definition: the share of an input cost increase recovered through contractual escalation clauses, and the elapsed time before the contract reprices.
  • Why it matters: in any model selling multi-year capacity against volatile inputs, the escalator is what stands between input inflation and margin. It is a defined contractual mechanism, not a pricing decision, so it can be measured rather than argued.
  • Two components, and both must be stated: the covered share (what proportion of revenue sits on contracts carrying an escalator at all) and the lag (how long before it resets).
  • Moves up: a higher term mix; escalator formulas indexed to a published series rather than negotiated; shorter reset periods.
  • Moves down: spot exposure, which typically carries no escalator; formula terms that under-recover; volatility within the lag window.
  • Where to find it: the contracts section of the 10-K, and the risk factors. Operators state the lag more readily than the capture rate.
  • Reference: Kirby Corporation FY2025 — marine term contracts "typically include fuel escalation clauses, or the customer pays for the fuel," with "generally a 30 to 120 day delay before contracts are adjusted"; spot contracts carry no fuel escalator, and spot was 30% of inland revenue. Separately, annual labour and PPI escalators on multi-year inland contracts produced rate increases of 3-5% effective 1 January 2025, excluding fuel. Grade ``.
  • Common misread: scoping the exposure to fuel. Fuel is the escalator most often disclosed, but labour and producer-price escalators sit on the same contracts and cover a far larger cost base. Sizing an escalator argument on fuel alone understates it.
  • Diagnostic question: "On the contracts that repriced last quarter, what did the formula give you against what the input actually did?"

Revenue per FEU (liner)

  • Definition: segment revenue divided by containers carried, in forty-foot equivalent units.
  • Why it matters: the yield measure for model 9, and the input to every cost-per-unit-of-work comparison.
  • Moves up: lane mix shifting toward premium services; rate increases; higher accessorial and demurrage capture.
  • Moves down: mix shifting toward lower-yield protected lanes; rate competition; fuel surcharge deflation.
  • Where to find it: compute from the disclosed FEU volume table and segment revenue. Neither is published as a rate.
  • Reference: Matson, Inc. Ocean Transportation, $7,005 per FEU in both FY2025 and FY2024. Grade `` for the inputs.
  • Two cautions on the arithmetic. Matson, Inc. states that volume is recorded on voyage departure date while revenue is adjusted for voyages in transit at period end, so numerator and denominator sit on different bases — the quotient is approximate and should be labelled. And an aggregate that is flat while lane mix moves materially is a mix coincidence, not a yield finding. Matson, Inc. FY2025 lost 9.5% of its highest-yield China volume and the average did not move. That implies per-lane yields rose. Lane-level yield is not disclosed, so it stays a question.
  • Common misread: treating group revenue per FEU as a price. It is a mix index across lanes with very different economics.
  • Diagnostic question: "What is the yield on the lane that lost volume, against the lane that gained it?"

Direct Cargo Expense per FEU (liner)

  • Definition: terminal handling, stevedoring, wharfage and outside purchased transportation, divided by containers carried.
  • Why it matters: the largest cost line in model 9 and, at Matson, Inc., the only one whose unit cost moved. It is where terminal and longshore cost inflation lands, and it is largely outside the carrier's operational control where terminals are run by a joint venture or a third party.
  • Moves up: terminal operator cost inflation; longshore wage settlements; lower lift volumes over a fixed terminal cost base; congestion.
  • Moves down: lift volume growth; terminal productivity; renegotiated stevedoring terms.
  • Where to find it: the segment cost table plus the FEU volume table. Where the terminal operator is a related party, the specific charge is disclosed separately in the related-party note.
  • Reference: Matson, Inc. FY2025 $2,559 per FEU against $2,444 in FY2024, +4.7% on volume down 2.6%. The SSAT component was $875 against $800, +9.4%; the non-SSAT remainder $1,683 against $1,643, +2.4%. Grade `` for the inputs.
  • Common misread: reading the aggregate direct cargo line as controllable operating cost. Split the related-party terminal charge out before drawing any conclusion. At Matson, Inc. it was 34.2% of the line, and carried all of the deterioration.
  • Diagnostic question: "What did your cost per lift do, and how much of that is the terminal operator's cost base rather than your volume?"

Billets and the Rotation Multiplier (liner)

  • Definition: a billet is a crew position on an active vessel. Each billet is filled by two or more employees, because seagoing personnel rotate between sea duty and time ashore.
  • Why it matters: it converts a sailing schedule into a headcount floor. Seagoing employment is an output of how many vessels sail on what rotation, not a discretionary cost line, so ordinary headcount levers do not reach it.
  • Moves up: more active vessels; longer strings; rotation patterns with more time ashore per sea day.
  • Moves down: vessels laid up or moved to reserve; chartered-in foreign-flag tonnage, where the vessel owner carries the crew.
  • Where to find it: the human capital section of the 10-K. Matson, Inc. discloses the billet count directly; most operators do not.
  • Reference: Matson, Inc. FY2025 — 329 billets on the active fleet, each "typically filled by two or more employees", within 4,170 employees of whom 2,843 (68%) are under collective agreements. Excludes foreign-flag chartered tonnage. Grade ``.
  • Common misread: reading a hiring freeze or a headcount reduction as a reduction in the cost of doing the work. It reaches the shoreside remainder only, and it changes when a cost lands rather than what it costs. See billets make seagoing headcount a schedule output, not a cost lever.
  • Diagnostic question: "How many of your people are billet-driven, and what is the rotation multiplier on each pattern?"

Deferred Dry-docking Payments (liner)

  • Definition: cash paid for regulatory dry-docking of ocean-going vessels, capitalised and amortised over the period to the next docking — generally 30 or 60 months. Regulation requires dry-docking twice in every five-year period.
  • Why it matters: it is the most deferrable cost in model 9 and therefore the one most likely to be presented as a saving when it has only been moved. The accounting separates cash paid from expense recognised, so the two lines can move in opposite directions in the same year.
  • Moves up: dockings falling due; shipyard rate inflation; deferred work arriving; older tonnage requiring more extensive scope.
  • Moves down: deferral, which is temporary by construction.
  • Where to find it: the cash flow statement and the liquidity discussion for payments; the balance sheet for deferred dry-docking costs, net; the D&A guidance for the amortisation component.
  • Reference: Matson, Inc. cash dry-docking payments $49.4m (2025) against $30.2m (2024), a 64% increase in the year in which maintenance capital guidance was cut by $20m. Deferred dry-docking costs, net, $94.7m at FY2025. FY2026 guidance approximately $45m of payments and approximately $35m of amortisation within approximately $210m of total D&A. Grade ``.
  • Common misread: treating the guidance cut as the cost outcome. Read the cash line, then the deferred asset balance. A falling guidance number against a rising cash number and a rising deferred balance is deferral, not reduction.
  • Diagnostic question: "How many dockings are past their planned window, and what does the yard quote now against what it quoted then?"

Finished-Goods Days (equipment distribution)

  • Definition: finished goods inventory divided by cost of sales, x 365. For a dealer or distributor, computed on the segment, never the group.
  • Why it matters: the working capital measure for an equipment distribution and service business, and the leading indicator of specification obsolescence in a market where product configurations move.
  • Moves up: extended supplier lead times against unchanged reorder points; order build-ahead; demand falling faster than the order pipeline unwinds; branch network expansion.
  • Moves down: demand exceeding supply; consignment; a written-down balance.
  • Where to find it: the inventories note, split into finished goods, work in process and raw materials, against the segment cost of sales line.
  • Reference: Kirby Corporation Distribution & Services FY2025 — finished goods $340.7m against segment cost of sales $1,045.4m = 119 days; total inventory $398.0m = 139 days. Grade ``.
  • The reading trap. Kirby Corporation's FY2025 10-K attributes the increase in inventory to power generation projects for 2026 delivery. The increase was $4.1m on a $393.9m base — inventory rose 1%. Reading the whole $340.7m finished-goods balance as stalled project stock reads a stock as if it were the flow the filing described. For a 62-branch parts and engine distribution network, 119 days of finished goods is working stock. See an attributed inventory increase does not characterise the balance.
  • Common misread: benchmarking a distributor's inventory days against a cross-industry working capital median. The valid comparison is dealer peers Finning, Toromont, Wajax, Alta Equipment.
  • Diagnostic question: "What is the ageing profile of finished goods, and how much of it is against a specification that has since moved?"

Segment Asset Turnover

  • Definition: segment revenue divided by segment total assets.
  • Why it matters: it is the fastest way to detect that a group's operating models are structurally different, and therefore that no consolidated ratio should be quoted. It is also the denominator half of return on capital, which margin analysis ignores.
  • Moves up: capital-light revenue mix; asset disposals; a depreciated asset base.
  • Moves down: newbuild and expansion capital landing ahead of the volume; acquisitions carried at fair value; a young fleet.
  • Where to find it: the segment note. Not every filer discloses segment assets — Kirby Corporation does; Matson, Inc. states that segment balance sheet information is not provided to the CODM and therefore publishes none.
  • Reference: Kirby Corporation FY2025 — Marine Transportation 0.41x ($1,935.3m over $4,705.7m), Distribution & Services 1.29x ($1,428.7m over $1,111.5m), group 0.56x. Pre-tax return on segment assets: Marine 8.0%, D&S 11.8%. Grade `` for the inputs.
  • What that pair changes. On operating margin, Kirby Corporation's marine segment (19.3%) looks like the better business and distribution (9.2%) the worse. On return on the assets each employs, the ranking inverts. A margin comparison across two models with a three-fold difference in asset turnover is not a performance comparison at all. See margin ranks segments backwards where asset turnover differs.
  • Common misread: quoting a consolidated SG&A, EBITDA or cash conversion ratio for a mixed-model group. Kirby Corporation FY2025 SG&A is 7.5% of revenue in marine and 14.1% in distribution; the consolidated 10.7% describes neither business and matches no peer set.
  • Diagnostic question: "Which segment holds the assets, and what does each earn on the capital it actually uses?"

Revenue per TEU (container liner)

  • Definition: liner revenue divided by loaded containers carried, in TEU.
  • Why it matters: it is the yield measure for international and regional container carriers. It must be read with lane mix and slot utilisation.
  • Moves up: rate increases, premium cargo mix, higher reefer or special cargo share, and stronger headhaul demand.
  • Moves down: spot-rate decline, mix toward feeder relay cargo, weak backhaul pricing, and empty repositioning.
  • Where to find it: compute from freight income and liftings where both are disclosed. Some carriers disclose average freight rate per TEU directly.
  • Reference: Regional Container Lines 2025 freight income THB 36,924m, with liftings up 215,547 TEU or 8.8%. The company stated Q4 2025 average freight rate fell 4.3% while liftings rose 3.7%. release dated 2026-03-27;.
  • Common misread: treating revenue per TEU as price. It is a lane and cargo mix index.
  • Diagnostic question: "Which lane added TEU, and did its yield sit above or below the network average?"

Time Charter Equivalent (TCE)

  • Definition: gross freight income less voyage costs, usually expressed in US dollars per day. Voyage costs include bunker, port and canal charges.
  • Why it matters: it converts voyage charters, time charters and COA voyages into a comparable ship-day number.
  • Moves up: higher freight rate, shorter ballast leg, slower fuel-efficient steaming where time cost is lower than bunker saving, and premium cargo or ice-class employment.
  • Moves down: bunker spikes on voyage-risk contracts, longer ballast legs, port congestion, and lower spot market indices.
  • Where to find it: dry bulk and tanker operator earnings releases, investor decks and MD&A. It is non-GAAP; use the company's reconciliation.
  • Reference: Pangaea Logistics Solutions Q4 2025 TCE $17,773 per day, 19% above the average Baltic Panamax, Supramax and Handysize indices.
  • Common misread: comparing TCE without checking available-day definition. Some companies use voyage days; others use available days.
  • Diagnostic question: "How many days in the denominator were loaded, ballast, waiting, or off hire?"

Contracted Asset-Days (energy shipping)

  • Definition: vessel or offshore-asset days committed under charter, availability, production, storage or service contracts.
  • Why it matters: it is the denominator for LNG carriers and FPSO/FSO assets whose revenue is contracted before the period begins.
  • Moves up: new long-term charters, FPSO transition into operation, contract extensions, and higher fleet employment.
  • Moves down: off-hire events, contract expiry, scheduled dry-docking and vessel disposal.
  • Where to find it: backlog tables, fleet employment schedules, offshore project updates and segment MD&A.
  • Reference: MISC Berhad FY2025 Offshore revenue rose to $451.3m and operating income to $121.3m after an FPSO transitioned into operation and an FSO insurance recovery. 2026-02-24; for financials and C for management explanation.
  • Common misread: treating a contracted gas or FPSO asset as a spot tanker. Most of the economics may already be fixed.
  • Diagnostic question: "Which asset-days are fixed under contract, and which are exposed to spot or renewal pricing?"

Revenue Days (Great Lakes marine)

  • Definition: days when a vessel is available and earning freight, charter, pool or COA revenue.
  • Why it matters: the Great Lakes fleet has a seasonal navigation window. Revenue days are the operating bridge between volume, dry-docking and earnings.
  • Moves up: earlier season opening, fewer dry-dockings, higher industrial cargo programmes, and larger fleet in employment.
  • Moves down: winter closure, planned dry-docking, ice, lock or Seaway disruption, and weak steel or construction demand.
  • Where to find it: MD&A segment commentary and results releases. It is often described as a driver rather than disclosed as a count.
  • Reference: Algoma Central FY2025 Domestic Dry-Bulk revenue rose 8% to CAD 405.1m, with volumes up 10% and revenue days up 11%. Ocean Self-Unloaders revenue fell slightly because five planned dry-dockings reduced revenue days. ;.
  • Common misread: annualising Q1 or Q4. Layup and winter work are part of the operating model.
  • Diagnostic question: "How many revenue days were lost to docking, ice, and customer demand separately?"

Charter-in Cost per Revenue Day

  • Definition: charter hire and related lease cost divided by revenue days, shipping days or deployed vessel-days, using the denominator the operator discloses.
  • Why it matters: chartered-in tonnage can look flexible until hire is fixed above the current freight market. This is the metric behind charter-in rate exposure locked at the wrong point in the cycle.
  • Moves up: long-chartered tonnage fixed near a market peak; larger vessel mix; lease accounting bringing more hire into the measured base.
  • Moves down: charter redelivery, sublet tonnage, shorter fixtures, lower market hire at renewal.
  • Where to find it: charter hire expense, lease notes and fleet employment tables. Where revenue days are not disclosed, do not compute a pseudo-rate.
  • Common misread: calling chartered-in capacity variable because it is not owned. The contract tenor decides variability.
  • Diagnostic question: "Which chartered ships are priced above today's market, and when can they be redelivered?"

Vessel Off-Hire Days

  • Definition: days when a vessel is not earning hire or revenue because it is unavailable, including dry-docking, repairs, surveys and technical failure where the charter or disclosure treats the days as off hire.
  • Why it matters: it separates rate-cycle weakness from physical availability. It is the denominator test for off-hire and technical downtime convert owned fleet into idle capital.
  • Moves up: dry-docking bunching, technical downtime, class surveys, casualty repairs, crew or spare-parts constraints.
  • Moves down: better maintenance planning, younger fleet, reserved yard slots, fewer survey events.
  • Where to find it: dry bulk and tanker releases, fleet status tables, revenue-day bridges and off-hire notes.
  • Reference: Pangaea Logistics Solutions Q2 2025 disclosure recorded 167 off-hire days from dry dockings; registered in benchmark register as a company observation..
  • Common misread: reading lower revenue as lower demand. Off-hire is a capacity denominator, not a price.
  • Diagnostic question: "How many owned ship-days were not billable, and which cause code removed them?"

Liner Schedule Reliability

  • Definition: share of scheduled vessel arrivals that arrive within the measurement provider's on-time window.
  • Why it matters: scheduled liner shipping sells a weekly service promise. When reliability falls, cost moves through extra ships, skipped calls, terminal costs and customer service failures.
  • Moves up: stable rotations, berth-window discipline, fewer port omissions, lower congestion and better recovery planning.
  • Moves down: port congestion, vessel bunching, weather, blank sailings, terminal labour disruption and route diversions.
  • Where to find it: Sea-Intelligence Global Liner Performance, Drewry schedule reliability products and carrier service reports. Public releases are usually global or carrier-level, not lane-specific enough for diagnosis.
  • Reference: Sea-Intelligence reported 59.0% global schedule reliability in February 2026 with 5.49 days average delay for late arrivals.;.
  • Common misread: treating low schedule reliability as an operator failure. Use trade-lane peer performance before asserting port congestion breaks liner rotation integrity.
  • Diagnostic question: "Which string missed the window against the trade's peer reliability, and what cost did the recovery add?"

Bunker and Carbon Cost Recovery

  • Definition: realised surcharge, bunker adjustment factor or carbon-charge recovery divided by bunker, diesel, EUA or other emissions-compliance cost in the same period.
  • Why it matters: fuel and carbon costs sit on different parties depending on voyage charter, time charter, bareboat charter and regulatory responsibility. This is the metric behind bunker and carbon pass-through clause mismatch.
  • Moves up: shorter reset periods, published surcharge tables, explicit ETS clauses, customer contracts tied to the same index as the operator's cost.
  • Moves down: spot exposure, long reset lags, mismatched indices, manual true-ups and reimbursement disputes.
  • Where to find it: surcharge tables, contract notes, fuel cost lines, ETS disclosures and management commentary. Most filings disclose the existence of clauses rather than the capture rate.
  • Reference: Kirby Corporation FY2025 discloses 30-120 day fuel escalator lag on term contracts and no fuel escalator on spot inland revenue;. EU ETS shipping responsibility and reimbursement rules are registered in benchmark register,.
  • Common misread: assuming a pass-through clause means full recovery. Covered share, reset lag and index match must be stated.
  • Diagnostic question: "What did the surcharge recover against the actual cost paid in the same quarter?"

Freight Cost per Unit Shipped

  • Definition: total freight costs divided by number of standard volume or weight units shipped (e.g. hundredweight, cases, or tonnes) in the measurement period.
  • Why it matters: measures baseline freight procurement efficiency independent of product sales value fluctuations.
  • Moves up: spot market rate spikes, carrier capacity deficits, smaller average shipment sizes, mode shifts toward expedites.
  • Moves down: shipment consolidation, volume procurement discounts, shift toward lower-cost modes (rail, intermodal).
  • Where to find it: internal shipper TMS logs, supply chain management accounting records, 3PL client quarterly business reviews.
  • Reference: transportation KPI register (2018-10-09) records baseline operational framing across shipper accounts.. [HISTORICAL 2018].
  • Common misread: confusing lower freight cost per unit with improved logistics performance when product density or shipment sizing changed.
  • Diagnostic question: "Did your freight cost per unit drop because purchasing negotiated lower lane rates or because load planning consolidated LTL into truckload?"

Outbound Freight Cost as % of Net Sales

  • Definition: outbound freight expenditures divided by net sales revenue.
  • Why it matters: isolates logistics cost friction on gross margin across commercial contracts and customer accounts.
  • Moves up: surcharges, rising spot rates, expedited shipments from manufacturing delays, geographical expansion into low-density markets.
  • Moves down: delivered pricing discipline, minimum order threshold increases, customer pickup programs, regional inventory staging.
  • Where to find it: corporate income statements, shipper cost of goods sold schedules, commercial contract margin reconciliations.
  • Reference: transportation KPI register (2018-10-09) marks standard shipper benchmark at 3.0% to 6.5% of net sales depending on commodity.. [HISTORICAL 2018].
  • Common misread: benchmarking across different product-value tiers. High-value electronics operate under 1.5%; bulk building products exceed 12.0%.
  • Diagnostic question: "Which customer accounts carry freight costs exceeding 8% of sales, and are those costs captured in product pricing?"

Transit Time Variance

  • Definition: actual elapsed transit time minus contracted or quoted transit time standard by lane.
  • Why it matters: delivery volatility drives buffer inventory holding costs, production line downtime, and customer penalty fees.
  • Moves up (worse): driver shortages, border crossing congestion, port gate bottlenecks, transfer terminal delays, adverse weather.
  • Moves down (better): dedicated carrier routing, automated milestone tracking, terminal bypass scheduling.
  • Where to find it: shipper TMS exception reports, carrier EDI 214 status messages, telematics location feeds.
  • Reference: transportation KPI register (2018-10-09) records standard allowable tolerance at ±2 hours for scheduled truckload appointments.. [HISTORICAL 2018].
  • Common misread: relying exclusively on carrier-reported on-time delivery percentages without verifying delivery appointment timestamp accuracy.
  • Diagnostic question: "How many transit days of safety stock does your supply chain hold to compensate for carrier delivery variance?"

Claims as % of Freight Spend

  • Definition: total dollar value of cargo loss and damage claims filed against carriers divided by total freight spend in the period.
  • Why it matters: signals cargo handling failure, improper packaging, or carrier operational negligence. Directly erodes carrier net revenue.
  • Moves up: rough handling at cross-docks, improper pallet securement, temperature excursions in reefer transit, theft.
  • Moves down: improved pallet wrapping and packaging standards, load securement audits, carrier selection filtering.
  • Where to find it: corporate risk management registers, carrier OS&D (over, short, and damaged) claims logs, 10-K risk disclosures.
  • Reference: transportation KPI register (2018-10-09) notes industry standard target below 1.0% of freight spend for general freight.. [HISTORICAL 2018].
  • Common misread: assuming zero claims indicates zero damage. Unfiled claims below deductible thresholds disguise chronic handling waste.
  • Diagnostic question: "What portion of damaged freight claims is written off internally rather than recovered from the carrier's cargo insurance?"

Routing Guide Compliance Rate

  • Definition: percentage of shipments tendered to and accepted by the primary contracted carrier specified in the shipper's routing guide.
  • Why it matters: routing guide compliance enforces negotiated contract rates. Non-compliance pushes volume to secondary carriers or spot markets.
  • Moves up: realistic contracted volume commitments, competitive contracted rates, transparent load tendering lead times.
  • Moves down: tender rejection by primary carriers, manual dispatch workarounds, short notice tenders, tight lane capacity.
  • Where to find it: shipper TMS tender reports, carrier procurement audit logs, logistics procurement post-mortems.
  • Reference: transportation KPI register (2018-10-09) cites 90% to 95% primary routing guide compliance as the target for stable freight networks.. [HISTORICAL 2018].
  • Common misread: treating 95% compliance as healthy when non-compliant 5% accounts for 40% of total lane cost variance.
  • Diagnostic question: "When your primary contracted carrier rejects a load, how many routing guide steps does the tender cascade through before spot coverage?"

Accessorial Charges as % of Freight

  • Definition: non-freight accessorial billing (detention, layover, re-delivery, lumper, liftgate fees) divided by base linehaul freight spend.
  • Why it matters: accessorial charges signal facility inefficiency and operational friction. They represent preventable cost leakage.
  • Moves up: excessive loading and unloading dock delays, driver detention beyond 2 hours, missing delivery paperwork, missed appointment windows.
  • Moves down: automated yard management, pre-loading trailers (drop-and-hook), strict dock scheduling, appointment compliance.
  • Where to find it: freight bill audit invoices, carrier freight bills, shipper accounts payable exception reports.
  • Reference: transportation KPI register (2018-10-09) notes benchmark accessorial spend between 2.0% and 5.0% of linehaul freight.. [HISTORICAL 2018].
  • Common misread: negotiating down linehaul rates while ignoring ballooning accessorial charges that erase the rate discount.
  • Diagnostic question: "What proportion of accessorial charges is accounted for by driver detention at your three worst-performing warehouse facilities?"

Tender Acceptance / Rejection Rate

  • Definition: percentage of load tenders accepted by contracted carriers versus tenders rejected within the required tender response window.
  • Why it matters: leading indicator of carrier capacity tightness and contractual rate durability. A spike in rejections precedes margin erosion.
  • Moves up (higher acceptance): competitive contract rates, driver-friendly facilities, predictable scheduling, balanced two-way lanes.
  • Moves down (higher rejection): spot market rates rising above contract rates, surge volumes exceeding commitment caps, excessive facility detention.
  • Where to find it: TMS electronic tender logs (EDI 990 responses), carrier procurement dashboards, FreightWaves SONAR indices.
  • Reference: transportation KPI register (2018-10-09) identifies primary tender acceptance above 92% as normal; below 85% signals contract breakdown.. [HISTORICAL 2018].
  • Common misread: interpreting high tender acceptance during a freight recession as carrier loyalty rather than absence of alternative market loads.
  • Diagnostic question: "What is your primary tender acceptance rate on outbound lanes during peak shipping months compared to annual average?"

Dock Turnaround and Dwell Time

  • Definition: average time elapsed from a vehicle's arrival at the facility gate to its departure following completion of loading or unloading.
  • Why it matters: directly determines asset utilisation for carriers and drives detention surcharges for shippers. Facilities exceeding 120 minutes suffer carrier boycotts or rate penalties.
  • Moves up: unpalletised floor loads, manual check-in processes, dock staffing shortages, staging delays, pallet shortages.
  • Moves down: appointment scheduling, automated gate check-in, drop-and-hook trailer operations, pre-staged outbound freight.
  • Where to find it: facility yard management systems (YMS), electronic logging device (ELD) geofence data, security gate logs.
  • Reference: transportation KPI register (2018-10-09) establishes 120-minute free-time standard before detention billing commences.. [HISTORICAL 2018].
  • Common misread: measuring only time at the loading dock door while omitting gate-in queue wait times.
  • Diagnostic question: "What is the true gate-to-gate dwell time for outside carriers at your distribution facilities versus your internal fleet?"
