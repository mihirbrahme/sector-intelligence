The exception lens

The question: where does the deviation go?

Apply this when diagnosing any mechanism, not only the ones about exceptions.

A value tree decomposes a metric into drivers, and each driver into a cost per unit of work times a volume of work. The lens splits that unit of work one level further, into the transactions that went to plan and the transactions that deviated, and then follows the deviation:

  • Who touches it? Which function picks up a load that missed its appointment, a file with a document mismatch, an invoice the customer rejected. Name the function, not the system.
  • How many times is it touched? A deviation that returns to the same desk three times is three units of cost, not one.
  • Does the cost scale with volume, or with the deviation rate? This is the test that matters. A cost that scales with the deviation rate does not fall when volume grows, and it does not fall when the happy path is automated.

Why it matters for reading a mechanism. Automation is usually applied to the path that goes to plan, because that path is specifiable. The deviating transactions keep their manual handling, so unit cost stops falling even though throughput rises. In the financials this reads as cost per unit of work resistant to volume scaling, and productivity metrics that plateau after an automation programme rather than during one. That plateau is the signature to look for — it is what separates a company that automated the transaction from one that automated the exception.

What must be sourced, never assumed. The share of transactions that deviate and the share of operational attention they consume are company-specific and vary by operating model, lane, and customer. They are diagnostic questions, not constants. Ask them; do not carry a ratio into a note.

Diagnostic question: "What share of your operations team's day goes to loads that did not go to plan?"

named failure.

The unit-of-work substitution anti-pattern

The claim: "the value is worth X% of this cost line."

Treat a value pool sized as a percentage of a revenue or cost ratio as an anti-pattern, and go looking for the unit of work it replaced.

A value tree ends in a cost per unit of work times a volume of work. When the unit of work is not established, the arithmetic has nowhere to land. The sizing then falls back on the only quantity available: a disclosed cost line multiplied by an estimated addressable share. The result looks like a value tree and carries a defensible-sounding baseline. It also cannot be reconstructed by the company from its own operating data, which is the test it fails.

The signature is a sentence of the form "Group G&A of $294.1m x 45% — our estimated transactional share", or "1.5 to 3.0 margin points on 43% of segment revenue". Two things are true of every instance:

  1. The estimated share carries most of the value and none of the evidence. It is the number a CFO attacks first, correctly, because it is the only one in the chain that was not observed.
  2. A computable unit-of-work figure usually existed and was not used. This is the part that matters. The substitution is rarely necessary.
The test: compute the unit cost and see which way it moved

Before accepting a cost-ratio pool, take the disclosed cost line, divide it by the disclosed volume of work, and compare two periods. Three outcomes:

  • Unit cost rose while the ratio fell. The ratio was tracking revenue, not work. The pool is real but is probably on a different line than the one proposed.
  • Unit cost was flat. There is no unit-cost problem on that line, whatever the ratio did. Sizing a pool there is sizing a number that did not move.
  • Unit cost fell. The line is improving and the ratio is a mix artefact.

A pursuit document sized its largest pool as 45% of Matson, Inc.'s $294.1m general and administrative line. It named that share as its own estimate, and as the assumption worth testing first. The volume of work — containers carried, in FEU — is disclosed, as is every major cost line. Computing them:

Line 2025 per FEU 2024 per FEU Change
General and administrative $753 $757 −0.5%
Vessel operating expense $1,521 $1,522 −0.1%
Operating overhead $843 $820 +2.7%
Direct cargo expense $2,559 $2,444 +4.7%
— of which the terminal JV $875 $800 +9.4%

Overhead as a ratio to revenue had risen from 5.8% to 8.5% since FY2021, which is what the document observed. Cost per unit of work on that same line was flat. The line that deteriorated was terminal handling, and within it the joint-venture terminal operator — the one the document explicitly declined to build on, on the correct ground that the company does not control it.

The finding is not that the document was careless. It reasoned from the ratio it could see to the conclusion the ratio supported. The unit cost was one division away and inverts the answer.

Why this is not the exception lens

The exception lens asks where the deviation goes once the unit of work is established. This anti-pattern is upstream of it: the unit of work was never established, so there is nothing to split. Run this test first; the exception lens is what you apply to whatever the unit turns out to be.

How to write it

Where a unit of work is disclosed, size on it. Where it is not, say the pool cannot be sized from outside, and name it as the first thing a diagnostic measures. Matson, Inc. does not publish deployed slot capacity, so load factor is not computable at all. That is a stronger position than a number resting on an estimated share, because it survives being checked.

Per, our inability to compute a unit cost is an observation of the disclosure, not a finding about the company. And per, a stated assumption does not license a figure the evidence cannot carry: the figure is what gets copied into the deck.

Diagnostic question: "What does it cost you to produce one of these today, and is that a number anyone here reports?"

Origin: review of the Kirby Corporation and Matson, Inc. pursuit deliverables, 2026-09-08. See cost ratios and cost per unit of work move in opposite directions.

The volume-rejection anti-pattern

The claim: "we walked away from unprofitable freight."

Treat this as an anti-pattern, not an observation. An operator losing share and an operator deliberately shedding low-margin freight produce identical disclosure. Both report volume down. Both report margin up or holding. Both attribute the volume decline to a choice. Nothing in the volume line, the margin line, or the commentary separates them, and the commentary is in every instance — it is the operator's account of their own decision, which is precisely the account they have the strongest reason to shape.

It is the standard management explanation for lost volume. Its frequency is a reason for suspicion, not for confidence.

What separates them

The decomposition is the same in both cases — revenue equals volume times yield — but the two stories make different predictions about the freight that stayed. Test the retained book, not the lost volume.

  1. Yield on retained freight, and whether it rose enough to pay for the volume given up. This is the arithmetic test and it comes first. Deliberate shedding is a trade: margin dollars per unit of work rise by more than the units lost. Compute total contribution — yield per unit times retained units — across the period. If total contribution fell, no volume was deliberately shed; freight was lost and the yield improvement is a mix artefact of losing the cheapest freight first. A company that chose this will show contribution flat or up. A company that lost will show contribution down and yield up, which is what losing your lowest-priced business looks like from the outside.
  2. Whether yield rose on freight the company kept, or only on the average. Shedding raises the average by removing the bottom of the distribution without any retained account repricing. Deliberate shedding paired with commercial strength shows repricing within retained accounts — same customers, same lanes, higher rate. Observable where rate per unit is disclosed by lane, segment or customer cohort rather than only in aggregate. Where only the aggregate is disclosed, say so; do not infer.
  3. Which freight left. Shedding is selective and its selection has a stated basis — a margin threshold, a service cost, a customer's payment behaviour. Losing share is not selective. If the volume decline is broadly proportional across lanes, segments and customer sizes, the selection story is not supported by the shape of the decline.
  4. Whether capacity was released with the volume. An operator who chose to shed sizes the network down, or redeploys the capacity, or holds it deliberately and says why. An operator losing share carries the capacity and the deleverage shows up as overhead per unit rising. Old Dominion Freight Line, Inc. is the instructive case in the sample: it held approximately 30% reserve door capacity across 257 service centres through the decline and said it was holding it as an option, then recovered 450bp of operating ratio on a 4.0% sequential tonnage move in 2026-Q2. That is a stated, priced choice with a later observable payoff — a different thing from a claim of choice with no capacity decision behind it.
  5. Whether the freight came back, and at what price. The strongest test and the slowest. Shed freight that returns at the operator's price confirms the shedding was a pricing decision. Lost freight returns at the market's price or does not return.
How to write it

Never repeat the operator's claim as a finding. Write what the retained book did. If the tests are not runnable on the available disclosure — most often because yield is disclosed only in aggregate — say the claim is untestable on this disclosure and leave it as a diagnostic question. Per, our inability to test it is an observation of our evidence, not a finding about the company.

Diagnostic question: "Take the freight you turned down last year — what was the margin on it, and what did the accounts you kept reprice at?"

See management claims of deliberate volume rejection require yield proof.

The releverage asymmetry in fixed-network models

The question: what is held reserve capacity worth?

Not a lens applied to a mechanism — a decomposition to reach for when reading any operator whose cost base is a fixed network of terminals, doors and fleet. See held reserve doors create operating releverage asymmetry.

Overhead as a share of revenue rises on falling volume over a fixed base, then falls faster than it rose when volume returns. The asymmetry is the point. Old Dominion Freight Line, Inc. across 2025-Q4 to 2026-Q2: operating ratio +80bp to 76.7%, then +80bp to 76.2%, then −450bp to 70.1% — decomposed as 230bp of direct cost and 220bp of overhead — on daily tonnage still −4.1% YoY but +4.0% sequentially. Labour fell from 49.2% of revenue to 43.1% on headcount −7.1%, managed through attrition and overtime rather than layoffs. Capex was then raised $115 million to $380.0 million once volume turned.

What the decomposition gives you. 450bp of margin recovered on a 4% sequential volume move sizes the option value of the reserve capacity that was held through the trough. That is the number to put against the cost of holding it. An operator who cut the network to protect the trough margin bought a lower trough cost and sold this option; an operator who held it did the reverse. Both are defensible; they are different bets and the disclosure shows which was made.

Do not open an M-number on it. What it changes is how to read a trough: overhead deleverage in a fixed-network operator is arithmetic, and asserting an operational failure from it requires showing the deleverage exceeded what the volume decline and the held capacity explain.

The cyclicality lens

The question: is this margin movement the market cycle or the operator?

Logistics earnings move violently with macro freight cycles. A rising tide conceals operational deterioration; a cyclical trough masquerades as execution failure. Applying a value tree without first separating market rate cycles from operator performance produces false mechanism attributions.

Cross-reference all rate observations against benchmark register before diagnosing.

The five tests to separate cycle from operator

  1. Spread vs. benchmark index across equivalent vintage. Compare the operator's realized yield (revenue per loaded mile, net revenue per load, or tariff per lift) against the appropriate benchmark index in benchmark register: DAT dry van spot/contract for truckload, Cass for aggregate volume/spend, Drewry WCI or Xeneta XSI for ocean, SCFI for spot export. - Cycle: The operator's yield moves in lockstep with the benchmark index. - Operator outperformance: Realized yield spread widens positively against the benchmark, confirming contract retention or pricing discipline. - Operator failure: Spread narrows faster than the market index, indicating panic discounting or unbilled accessorial leakage (billing exception leakage).

  2. Deadhead / empty mile ratio vs. corridor imbalance. Corridor directional imbalance fluctuates with macro trade flows (e.g. West Coast US import swings or Midwest agricultural harvest cycles). - Cycle: Empty miles increase across all regional peers due to macro freight flow imbalances. - Operator failure: Empty mile percentage rises significantly faster than peer averages, signaling poor fleet dispatch or local sales imbalance.

  3. Customer-level contract rate retention vs. lane-level market drift. When market rates drop, shippers demand contract renegotiations or tender compliance falls. - Cycle: Widespread tender rejection across the sector as shippers tender into the falling spot market. - Operator failure: Loss of contracted rate integrity driven by account-level service failures, billing disputes (billing exception leakage), or unmanaged freight classification drift.

  4. Capacity utilisation vs. deliberate fleet sizing. In a freight downturn, asset operators face falling equipment utilisation. - Cycle: Broad industry decline in loaded miles per seated tractor per week. - Operator failure: High unseated tractor ratio (>8–10%) caused by driver turnover following compensation cuts (post-restructuring capability gap), or delayed fleet disposal creating unabsorbed depreciation.

  5. Purchased transportation cost flex and lag. Non-asset brokers and intermodal marketing companies purchase capacity from underlying asset carriers. - Cycle: In falling markets, purchased transportation cost drops faster than contract shipper pricing, mechanically expanding gross margins (the broker windfall). In rising markets, purchased transportation spikes immediately, squeezing margins. - Operator failure: Inability to capture buy-side drops during a loose market due to manual carrier sourcing (manual carrier sourcing under rate volatility), or severe margin compression caused by contractual minimum volume commitments on rail linehaul agreements.

Ocean and Marine Addendum

Ocean rates move far enough that a weak mechanism can look proved by the cycle. Run these tests before using charter-in rate exposure locked at the wrong point in the cycle through bunker and carbon pass-through clause mismatch.

  1. Yield versus market index. Compare revenue per TEU, TCE, day-rate renewal or revenue per FEU with the specific lane or vessel-class index. Use SCFI, Drewry WCI, FBX, Baltic dry bulk or tanker indices from benchmark register. - Cycle: company yield moves with the index. - Mechanism: company yield underperforms the index, or unit cost rises while index-adjusted yield is stable.

  2. Rate effect versus denominator effect. Split the change into price, volume and days. Dry-docking, off-hire and port congestion remove days or rotations; the freight cycle changes price. - Cycle: TCE, freight rate or day rate moves, while available days hold. - Mechanism: available days, revenue days, ship-days or schedule rotations fall faster than the market volume indicator.

  3. Owned cost versus chartered-in cost. Owned ships create depreciation, dry-docking and debt service. Chartered-in ships create hire or lease expense. - Cycle: both revenue and market charter rates move together. - Mechanism: charter hire per day or per TEU stays high after revenue yield falls. That is charter-in rate exposure locked at the wrong point in the cycle.

  4. Bunker and carbon cost versus freight rate. Fuel and carbon cost need their own index. Do not infer pass-through failure from a margin move. - Cycle: yield follows the freight-rate index. - Mechanism: realised surcharge or yield lags bunker, diesel or EUA cost after controlling for freight-rate indices. That is bunker and carbon pass-through clause mismatch or escalator and surcharge recovery lag under input inflation.

  5. Schedule reliability versus price. A liner can have high revenue because the market is tight while its schedule is deteriorating. Those facts can coexist. - Cycle: rates rise with sector congestion, and the operator's schedule reliability is in line with peers. - Mechanism: the operator underperforms trade-lane schedule reliability, adds vessels to preserve weekly service, skips ports, or shows cost per TEU rising from delay. That is port congestion breaks liner rotation integrity.

  6. Regulatory deadline versus yard-price cycle. Environmental compliance capex needs a rule date, vessel class and affected asset list. - Cycle: vessel values or yard prices move without a rule-linked fleet action. - Mechanism: retrofit, sale, speed limitation or scrapping lines up with EEXI, CII, EU ETS or other rule timing. That is environmental compliance capex forces retrofit, speed or scrap decision.

Diagnostic question: "Did your gross margin change because market buy rates moved, or did your team beat the DAT/Drewry benchmark on the lanes you bought?"

Standard value trees by operating model

One tree per operating model, decomposing that model's primary profitability metric into the operational drivers that move it.

A gap is not an invitation to write a mechanism — it is a place to look. Several of them are gaps for good reason: mix, cyclicality and fixed-network deleverage move these trees without any operational failure behind them, and a mechanism written over one of them would be an arithmetic identity dressed as a diagnosis.

8. Inland liquid-bulk marine / tank barge

Metric at the top: segment operating income, read together with return on segment assets. Margin alone is not sufficient in a model running at 0.4x asset turnover.

Marine operating income ├── Revenue │ ├── Delay-exposed revenue = inland revenue x (1 − term% x time-charter%) │ │ ├── Available barge-days ← fleet size, dry-dock and inspection days out │ │ ├── Utilisation ← demand: refinery and petrochemical volumes │ │ └── Rate per movement ← term renewal % and spot %, quarterly │ └── Delay-insulated revenue = inland term x time-charter%, plus coastal │ └── Day rate x days on hire ← delay does not reduce this line ├── Vessel operating cost │ ├── Crew ← billets by rotation pattern, licensed mariner supply │ ├── Fuel ← gallons x price, less escalator capture at 30-120 day lag │ ├── Chartered horsepower ← the flex lever, ~25% of requirement historically │ └── Maintenance and USCG inspection ← expensed inland, deferred coastal ├── Depreciation ~11% of segment revenue, and capex runs at ~11-12% └── SG&A ~7.5% of segment revenue

Where the tree bites. Three of these branches are demand or weather, and one is contractual. Four things the operator actually chooses: how much horsepower to charter; which vessel to which yard in which slot; how work is bundled into a visit; and the term/spot and affreightment/time-charter mix. Everything else in the tree is a price or a volume it does not set.

The mistake this tree is built to prevent. Applying a delay-day improvement to total segment revenue. Split the revenue line by contract form first; on Kirby Corporation FY2025 that halves the base. See model 8 in operating models and time charters make delay a cost event, not a revenue loss.

Quantities that must be sourced, not assumed: the term and time-charter mix (disclosed), the delay-day count and its causes (disclosed, and scoped to external causes by definition), yard slot availability (not disclosed), and the split of maintenance capital between planning-governed and price-governed components (not disclosed anywhere located).

9. Protected liner shipping / Jones Act non-contiguous trades

Metric at the top: segment operating income, and cost per FEU by line. It is not computable by segment where segment assets are not disclosed, which is the common case. Say so.

Ocean operating income ├── Revenue = FEU carried x revenue per FEU, by lane │ ├── FEU carried │ │ ├── Slot capacity deployed ← strings x sailings x slots [not disclosed] │ │ └── Load factor ← headhaul / backhaul balance [not disclosed] │ └── Revenue per FEU ← lane mix; protected lanes and premium lanes differ ├── Direct cargo expense per FEU ← the line that moves; split related-party out │ ├── Terminal and stevedoring ← JV or third-party operated; labour agreement │ └── Outside purchased transportation ├── Vessel operating expense per ship-day │ ├── Crew ← billets x rotation multiplier; a schedule output, not a lever │ ├── Fuel ← burn per ship-day x price, less surcharge capture and lag │ └── Repairs, stores, insurance ├── Operating overhead ← includes dry-docking amortisation, not the cash ├── Depreciation ← rises by design as newbuild tonnage delivers └── Equity income from terminal JV ← sits inside costs; strip it before reading margin

Where the tree bites. The top two branches are the return-on-capital question and neither is disclosed. The third is the line that actually deteriorated at Matson, Inc. and is the one the carrier least controls. The branches that are disclosed and controllable — overhead and G&A — are the ones whose unit cost did not move.

Two arithmetic requirements before reading margin.

  1. Strip the terminal joint-venture equity income out of segment operating income. Matson, Inc. records its share of SSAT income inside costs and expenses in the Ocean Transportation segment. FY2025 segment operating income of $455.6m includes +$32.5m of it; FY2024's $500.9m includes −$1.0m, itself carrying an $18.4m impairment share. Ex-JV: $423.1m against $501.9m, a 15.7% decline against the 9.0% headline. A joint venture recovering from a write-down absorbed 43% of the reported fall.
  2. Do not compute a broker-style net revenue for the logistics segment unless purchased transportation is disclosed separately. At Matson, Inc. it is not: the $494.0m "direct operating costs" line bundles purchased transportation with agency commissions, warehouse and cross-dock leases and facility operating costs. Deducting it gives $115.0m, which is not net revenue and understates it by the facility component. The honest output is that the segment's margin cannot be placed in a frame with C.H. Robinson or RXO, because requires net revenue and the disclosure does not support computing it. State that; do not produce the comparison and caveat it.

Quantities that must be sourced, not assumed: slot capacity and load factor (not disclosed by any operator located), lane-level yield (not disclosed), purchased transportation within logistics direct costs (not disclosed), and schedule reliability (published as a commitment, not as performance).

What the gaps say about the library

Counting terminal nodes across all seven trees:

  • Brokerage: 6 of 9 carry a mechanism.
  • Forwarding: 5 of 10.
  • Contract logistics: 4 of 11.
  • LTL: 4 of 13.
  • Asset carriage (truckload): 3 of 13. Uncovered nodes are fleet capex, fuel efficiency, deadhead, and self-insurance reserves.
  • Ports and terminals: 2 of 9. Uncovered nodes are concession structure, crane maintenance, and yard slot rehandling.
  • Domestic intermodal: 3 of 11. Uncovered nodes are rail contractual MVCs, container turn velocity, and unbilled empty repositioning.

The pattern: across all seven operating models, the library is concentrated in commercial pricing exceptions (manual carrier sourcing under rate volatility, billing exception leakage, detention and demurrage under-recovery), carrier settlement/accrual mismatches (carrier-selection liability repricing the cost of cheap capacity, purchased-transportation accrual failure), and headcount-cut consequences (post-restructuring capability gap). Physical network drivers (empty miles, berth congestion, container turns, rail MVCs) remain largely unwritten as formal mechanisms.
