Depreciated book flatters return on capital in long-lived marine fleets

For fleets of 25-to-40-year assets built over decades, reported return on capital sits on a denominator well below replacement cost. It therefore overstates the economic return.

Unit of workthe crewed towboat-day

In 90 seconds

Core takeaway

For fleets of 25-to-40-year assets built over decades, reported return on capital sits on a denominator well below replacement cost. It therefore overstates the economic return.

Operating models affected

Inland liquid-bulk marine / tank barge, Protected liner shipping / Jones Act non-contiguous trades

Where observed

kirby

Claim. For fleets of 25-to-40-year assets built over decades, reported return on capital sits on a denominator well below replacement cost. It therefore overstates the economic return. A marine operator earning approximately its cost of capital on book is earning less than that on the capital it must actually commit.

Why. Depreciation runs on original cost. Where construction prices have risen materially over the asset life — steel, shipyard labour — accumulated depreciation is measured against a cost base that no longer exists. The fleet still has to be replaced at today's price, so the capital genuinely employed is closer to replacement cost than to net book.

What was seen. Kirby Corporation FY2025, Form 10-K filed 2026-02-17, grade ``. A new 30,000-barrel inland tank barge costs approximately $4.5m; steel is disclosed as near historical highs; the national inland tank barge fleet averages approximately 18 years against hull lives of 25-40 years. Group total assets were $6,008.0m, of which the marine segment held $4,705.7m; group net property and equipment was $4,098.1m.

Replacing 1,105 inland tank barges alone at approximately $4.5m each is of the order of $5bn. That is comparable to the entire group book asset base. Marine segment operating income of $374.5m over segment assets of $4,705.7m gives 8.0% on book; on any denominator near replacement cost it is materially lower.

Note the direction, because it is the reverse of the LTL case. In LTL, terminal land at decades-old historic cost understates an appreciating, irreplaceable asset, so reported return is flattered by a denominator that undervalues something the operator will never buy again. Here the denominator undervalues something the operator must buy again. Both flatter the ratio; only one flatters the economics.

The measurement error on the denominator, and on any sector WACC proxy, is wider than the spread itself. State it as "approximately at the cost of capital, with no cushion, on a denominator that understates the replacement bill." Ask for the fleet age profile and the current newbuild price before quoting a return figure at all.
