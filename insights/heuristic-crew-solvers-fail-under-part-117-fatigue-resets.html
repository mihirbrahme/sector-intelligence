Heuristic crew solvers fail under Part 117 fatigue resets

Linear and heuristic crew scheduling algorithms collapse during major irregular operations because FAA Part 117 rest rules enforce non-linear, path-dependent resets that require combinatorial re-optimization.

Core takeaway

Linear and heuristic crew scheduling algorithms collapse during major irregular operations because FAA Part 117 rest rules enforce non-linear, path-dependent resets that require combinatorial re-optimization.

Why it is true

Under FAA Part 117 regulations (14 CFR Part 117), flight crew legal Flight Duty Periods (FDP) are governed by multi-dimensional tables based on circadian report time and segment count.

A pilot reporting at 06:00 for a two-segment day has a 13-hour maximum duty window. Adding an unexpected third segment dynamically reduces that duty limit to 12 hours.

If a gate hold pushes duty past the threshold, the pilot's legal clock immediately resets. The crew member must enter a mandatory 10-hour uninterrupted rest period before flying any subsequent leg.

Heuristic crew software attempts to repair schedules sequentially, patching individual broken flight legs one by one.

Each sequential patch causes path-dependent legality failures across downstream multi-day pairings. Schedulers assign relief crews who immediately "time out" on the taxiway, accelerating cancellations across the network.

What was seen

Observed in airline disruption and IROPS recovery and crew legality cascade under irregular operations.

During the December 2022 winter storm, Southwest Airlines cancelled over 16,700 flights. Its legacy scheduling solver, SkySolver, became computationally exhausted under cascading Part 117 resets. Crew scheduling resorted to manual phone queues with multi-hour hold times, leading to an unprecedented $140 million DOT civil penalty (DOT Docket OST-2023-0181).

During the July 2024 global CrowdStrike outage, Delta Air Lines cancelled over 5,000 flights over five days because crew tracking software could not reconcile pilot legalities, while United and American recovered within 48 hours.

DOT BTS on-time records confirm that second-day cancellations in major airline meltdowns are driven primarily by missing legal flight crews, not out-of-position aircraft.

What it changes

When auditing airline operations control centers or evaluating crew recovery software: 1. Never evaluate crew pairing platforms solely by nominal planned cost or sunny-day solver efficiency. 2. Demand stress-testing of crew re-optimization engines under scenarios where 20% of flights suffer rolling two-hour ground delays. 3. Decouple multi-day pilot rotations during seasonal storm windows, establishing isolated flying rings that contain cascading Part 117 timeouts within single hub regions.
