A disclosed metric definition can exclude the component a value case rests on

Read the operator's own definition of a disclosed operational metric before building on it. The filing that publishes a metric can scope it to exclude precisely the driver an improvement proposition aims at.

Claim. Read the operator's own definition of a disclosed operational metric before building on it. The filing that publishes a metric can scope it to exclude precisely the driver an improvement proposition aims at. The number will still look like it contains that driver.

Why. Operators define disclosed operational metrics for a purpose of their own, usually to explain revenue variance to investors. A metric built to explain what the operator could not control is scoped to causes it does not control. The number then travels into third-party analysis stripped of its definition.

What was seen. Kirby Corporation discloses delay days annually. FY2025: 11,410, down 1% from 11,583. The same 10-K defines it: "Delay days measure the lost time incurred by a tow (towboat and one or more tank barges) during transit when the tow is stopped due to weather, lock conditions, or other navigational factors." Grade ``, filed 2026-02-17.

Every disclosed cause is external and in transit. Yard scheduling, crew availability, work bundling and maintenance sequencing are not in the number. A proposition to "decompose delay days into controllable and uncontrollable" asks the metric to carry a category its definition excludes — the split does not exist inside it, and the company cannot produce it either without building a different measure.

Two further definitional traps in the same figure. The denominator is the tow, not the barge: 11,410 over an average 279 inland towboats is 40.9 days per towboat-year, 11.2% of calendar, where dividing by 1,105 barges gives 10.3 and understates it fourfold. And in a metric whose disclosed drivers are weather and lock infrastructure, a 1% year-on-year move is a mild winter, not management performance.

What it changes. Before using any disclosed operational metric as a baseline, quote its definition from the filing. Then check three things: what causes it admits, what its denominator is, and whether the proposed improvement sits inside its scope. Where it does not, say so, and name the measure that would — usually one the company does not yet keep.
