Consortium governance deadlocks inter-competitor data sharing

Centralized platform consortia in logistics fail from commercial distrust over proprietary customer and rate data, not from technical limitations of the data-sharing protocol.

Unit of workthe shipment file

In 90 seconds

Core takeaway

Centralized platform consortia in logistics fail from commercial distrust over proprietary customer and rate data, not from technical limitations of the data-sharing protocol.

Operating models affected

Freight Forwarding / NVOCC, Ports & Terminals

Where observed

05-solutions/failure history/consortium-blockchain-trade-documentation

Why it is true

In asset-heavy and non-asset logistics oligopolies (such as ocean container shipping in operating model 2), carrier profit margins depend on customer relationship control, proprietary lane yields, and capacity positioning.

When a dominant industry participant sponsors or co-develops a centralized data platform or distributed ledger, competing carriers view the platform as an intelligence-gathering tool for their rival.

Even if cryptographic access controls are technically robust, competitors refuse to route live manifest, shipper identity, or cargo value data through infrastructure managed by a competitor.

Governance deadlock ensues: rival carriers demand neutral consortium ownership, separate operational management, and veto power over data schema.

What was seen

Documented in consortium blockchain trade documentation.

TradeLens was co-developed by Maersk and IBM. Rival ocean carriers (MSC, CMA CGM, Hapag-Lloyd) initially refused to participate due to governance and commercial data privacy concerns.

Even after governance restructuring in 2019–2020 allowed rivals to join, competitors withheld commercial trade documents.

The inability to achieve neutral industry-wide participation led Maersk and IBM to decommission the platform in 2022–2023 after five years of development.

What it changes

Never propose or invest in a proprietary industry data-sharing consortium initiated by an active market competitor.

Evaluate collaborative logistics technologies using governance criteria: 1. Is the data protocol managed by an independent, non-commercial standards body (such as DCSA or UN/CEFACT) rather than a commercial vendor-carrier joint venture? 2. Does the architecture use open, decentralized REST APIs rather than proprietary walled-garden ledgers? 3. Can participants exchange data point-to-point without routing through a centralized competitor-monitored hub?
